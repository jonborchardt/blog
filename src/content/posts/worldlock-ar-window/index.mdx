Point your phone west from Seattle and there's a glowing tower rising out of Tacoma, labeled `Tacoma 42.4 km`. Pan up and the night side of the Earth curves away from you, city lights and all, with NYC's tower readable 3,815 km over the horizon. A white dash crosses the sky where the ISS actually is, right now.

Source: [github.com/jonborchardt/worldlock](https://github.com/jonborchardt/worldlock)

That's WorldLock: a native Android AR app that georegisters content to the real world using ARCore's Geospatial API. Not "AR" as in a dinosaur on your coffee table — AR as in the phone knows where it is and which way it's pointing to within a few meters and degrees, so a tower drawn over a city 3,800 km away lines up with where that city actually is.

## What's in it

- **City towers.** A default set (Seattle, Tacoma, Spokane, Missoula, NYC, Tokyo, …) plus any city you add by name — it geocodes and drops a tower. Seven render styles, from a Tron-shell prism to a shrine beam to a "data pillar" with scrolling bands and rotating collar rings, all done in one shader with a style table. Bases sit on real terrain via ARCore terrain anchors.
- **The whole Earth.** The globe isn't a mesh — it's a per-fragment ray-cast against a sphere through local sea level (deliberately not the true ellipsoid; the upgrade path lives in a `ponytail:` comment), drawn as one fullscreen triangle. Blue Marble day and night textures, blended at the real solar terminator computed from the current time. Tessellating a planet-sized sphere near the camera is a precision disaster; a ray that solves the quadratic in the fragment shader is analytic at every distance — the constant term is precomputed in doubles on the CPU, leaving only a possible float shimmer right at the horizon.
- **The live ISS.** Position fetched every 3 seconds from wheretheiss.at, linearly extrapolated between fixes, drawn as a little truss-and-solar-wings model that scales with distance so it stays visible at 400+ km.

## The one hard rule

Everything interesting in this app is a coordinate-precision problem wearing a trench coat. Earth-centered coordinates are ~6.4 million meters from the origin; 32-bit floats have ~7 significant digits; do the math and your geometry jitters by meters. So the rule is: authoritative positions are ECEF in **doubles**, converted to camera-relative ENU in doubles, and only the final origin-relative meters ever touch a GL float. The render origin stays pinned near you, where float precision is millimeters. The geodetic math even polices itself: a startup self-check verifies the conversions against exact WGS84 reference points and crashes the app at launch if they ever break.

The other quiet trick is the reference anchor: the first time the Geospatial pose is accurate enough (position < 15 m, yaw < 15°), the app plants an ARCore geospatial anchor at that pose and rides it from then on. Georegistration survives pause/resume and works indoors after the first good fix, instead of waiting for VPS to re-converge every time.

There's a looser render gate in front of that (yaw < 25°, with 2× hysteresis so it doesn't flicker), so towers appear early and swing into place while the anchor waits for the tight fix:

## The stats that matter

The entire app is **8 Kotlin files, about 1,900 lines**, with **one AR dependency** (`com.google.ar:core`) plus the Play Services location library that ARCore's Geospatial mode requires. No Android Studio was involved at any point — it's built, deployed, and debugged entirely from the command line. How that works, and why the codebase stayed that small, are the next two posts.
