Not everything I built at PrecisionDemand and AOL was a product. Some of it was just me, handed a
new pile of data, building the smallest interface that would let me look at it. None of these four
shipped to a customer. All four exist because I could not understand a dataset by staring at rows
of it, and building a small explorer was faster than not understanding it.

<Callout variant="note" title="Every number below is demo data">
  The ad counts, brands, package costs, audience counts, and pacing numbers in all four tools
  below are demo data, built to exercise the tools — not a real campaign.
</Callout>

## Adsplore: scoring 10,739 ads for fit

Adsplore is a wizard: pick some brands, pick some target audiences, and it hands you every TV ad
those brands ran, scored from −1 to +1 for how well it targeted the population you picked. One run
in the screenshots below pulled 10,739 ads. Crossfilter-style charts break the set down by
daypart, network, market, program, genre, and where the ad sat in its commercial pod, and clicking
any of them filters all the others live.

granularity — not something a client clicked through themselves.

## Package Compare: putting media buys side by side

Package Compare answers a narrower question: given two or more candidate media packages, which one
actually performs better against the numbers that matter — targetedness, spend, impressions by
channel, daypart, CPM, eCPM, GRPs? The tool lets a planner build several packages from an inventory
list, adjust cost and impression numbers per line, and see every package compared on the same set
of charts at once.

## Population Reporting: total people vs. total buyers

This one compares two curves across four demographic cuts — age in two-year increments, education
level, household income, and home market value — one line for how a population is distributed,
another for how buyers of a given target are distributed against it. The field names underneath
(`Income-EstimatedHouseh…`, `HomeMarketValue`) trace back to the same licensed consumer data,
Acxiom-lineage, that powered targeting elsewhere in the platform. The other screens in the set are
per-target reports — key totals and a tRatio distribution for a single target like PMIC Life — a
Target Planner with gender and ethnicity donuts over an age histogram, and vehicle-ownership charts
above the raw population table.

Where the other three tools are about a campaign or a buy, this one is about the audience itself —
a way to see whether a target population actually looks different from the general population, and
where.

## Campaign Reporting: pacing against a plan

The last of the four tracks delivery against a media plan while a campaign is running: weekly
impressions stacked as confirmed, preliminary, and projected, against an expected line the plan
committed to. The capture below steps through the weekly reports from 5/5/2017 to 8/4/2017, so you
can watch grey projected bars turn orange and then blue as the flight actually delivers — squarely
inside the ONE TV era at AOL.

## A detour, August 2014

One more, from the same instinct but a different occasion. Right after AOL bought PrecisionDemand,
there was an Adap.tv hackathon, and we built a thing called Soda War: a D3 map of the United States
with a circle at every market, sized by spend, red for Coke and blue for Pepsi, and a slider that
ran twenty-four hours of it forward. I did the front end; Sawin Lee, Dyng Au and Brendan Kitts did
the data. Whether we won it, I genuinely do not remember. The page still runs, so rather than a
screenshot, here is the page itself — press Play, or drag the slider yourself:

how I answered my own question, "what does this data actually look like?", before building the
thing that used it; Soda War was the same instinct with a scoreboard and a deadline attached.
