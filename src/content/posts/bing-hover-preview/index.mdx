Attenex was six years on one product. Bing was two years inside a machine that already shipped
to hundreds of millions of people a day, and it's where I learned what that scale actually does
to frontend work.

## What I built

I joined Microsoft in March 2008 as an SDE2 on the Bing User Experience team and stayed until
March 2010. Three things I built shipped to the live results page: the right-side hover preview
panel, which gave people an instant content preview without leaving the results list; the
quick-answer module at the top of results, which surfaced a direct answer before anyone needed
to click through; and a run of core features on the results list itself, the part of the page
nearly every query touches. All three sat in front of the primary traffic of one of the world's
largest search engines, which was a different kind of pressure than anything at a startup —
there was no slow ramp-up of users to catch a bad interaction before it mattered.

The stack underneath was C++, JavaScript, AJAX, and CSS — the vocabulary of pre-framework
frontend work, where you built and instrumented most of your own plumbing. Alongside the
shipping work I did rapid prototyping of UX ideas that weren't committed to yet, sat in design
and code reviews, and worked closely with designers and content producers to get a feature
from mockup to something that survived contact with real query traffic.

## The other first

Bing was also where I mentored for the first time — three junior engineers, formally assigned
to me. It wasn't something I'd done before and it wasn't something I was chasing; it came with
the scale of the team. It stuck, though: a lasting habit of trying to develop the people around
me followed from it, all the way through the teams I later led.

## What I'd tell that version of me

The hover preview panel is the one people bring up most when I describe this job, probably
because it changed a physical habit — you stopped needing to click away from the results to see
whether a page was worth opening. It's a small interaction by today's standards, but it was one
of the first times I watched a UI decision play out across a userbase too large to picture.
That scale is the throughline into everything else in this series: PrecisionDemand and AOL were
smaller audiences, but I never stopped designing as if someone would find the edge case.
