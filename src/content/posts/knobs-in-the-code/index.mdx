The Compose page is one source string with three views on it. You can type in the source. You can
drag a slider in the Mix card. You can hold alt and drag a number inside the code. You can click a
chord button, click a step in a drum grid, or pick a sample and hear it before choosing. Every one of
those is the same kind of edit: it goes into the file, it is unsaved until you save, it lands on one
undo stack, and if the song is playing it re-evaluates in place so you hear it land.

The four things that do not work that way are named in the interface as "what you hear, not the
song": solo, mute, the A/B button and the preview level. They never touch the file.

That is the whole design. The two posts before this one
covered [the language](/a-language-on-top-of-strudel/) the page edits and
[the words](/say-darker/) it accepts; this one is about the mouse.

## The page, top to bottom

- **The song header and transport.** Pick a song, press play. Pause remembers the cycle; stop
  forgets it. A readout shows the section, bar and chord under the playhead, and a small scope shows
  the peak in dBFS, held for a second, red at clipping. Space plays and pauses outside text fields.
- **The Mix card**, which is most of the page and most of this post: a song pane, an arrangement
  strip, a section pane, a harmony strip, an axes grid, a materials list and a phrase row.
- **The song source**, a code editor over the whole file, with a checkbox that puts a widget after
  every literal the language knows.
- **The expanded Strudel**, read-only, regenerated a third of a second after each edit: the plain
  Strudel the file reduces to, with the lines your last edit changed flashing.
- **Change comments** and **Why it sounds this way**: listening notes pinned to the transport, and
  the recorded reason behind every past change.

The editor puts controls **inside the code**. A slider sits after a number, a menu after a sound
name, a row of chord buttons after a progression. Holding alt and dragging a number changes it in
place, at 200 pixels for its full range, and that works whether or not the controls are switched on.

But only for literals a schema describes. And the schema is a whitelist, which means the default is
silence:

> A literal without an entry here never gets a control, so a wrong range cannot be invented for it.

The distinction between a slider and a spinner is the same principle one level down. A number with a
known ceiling gets a slider. A number with only a floor gets a spinner, because **a slider would have
to invent a maximum**, and inventing a maximum is lying about the language at sixty frames a second
under someone's cursor.

## One number, and what it costs to be wrong about it

The drum part in one section sits at `density: .9`. Drag it to `.84`, which is about twelve pixels
of mouse movement:

<SoundBite
  label="Two bars of drums, one value apart"
  note="density .9 against .84. The clap crosses its threshold and leaves the part."
  clips={[
    { src: dragBefore, label: "density .9" },
    { src: dragAfter, label: "density .84" },
  ]}
/>

Twenty-eight onsets per bar become twenty-six, and the section's energy drops from 59 to 57, because
voices join a drum part as density rises and the clap's threshold is 0.85. Six hundredths of a knob
is a voice appearing or disappearing.

Fixing that in a text file costs a save and a listen. Fixing it with a drag costs nothing, and you
hear it while the song is still playing.

## The Mix card

The card shows one section at a time: the one under the playhead, or the one you have pinned.

**The arrangement strip** is a block per section, as wide as the section is long, with one row per
part shaded by how dense and how loud it is. Click a block to scrub to it. Pin it and it loops on
its own, and play starts there. Each block has buttons to move it earlier or later, duplicate it,
rename it, or remove it, and a lightning pick that applies one of the arrangement verbs from the
previous post to the whole section. The gap between two blocks carries the transition: a riser of
so many bars at the end of this section, a hit sample on the downbeat of the next, and a dropout or
a filter sweep over the last bars.

**The harmony strip** shows the section's key and one button per bar with the chord's name over its
numeral. Each is a pick. A progression written with a hand-held `@n` repeat is shown read-only here,
because one bar in a picker cannot stand for a written repeat count.

**The axes grid** is a slider per axis per part, with an empty cell wherever that kind of part has no
implementation for that axis. An axis whose value is a signal is drawn as a lane instead, the actual
curve sampled across the section's bars, and clicking it opens the same shape pick every continuous
axis has: constant, ramp up, ramp down, wobble, drift, pulse, swell, each written centred on the
current value. Under every row an event strip draws the part's onsets across the bars, with a
playhead sweeping over it while that section sounds.

**Materials** is one row per part for everything that is not an axis. A drum part shows its template
as a pick, or, once you have chosen to write it out, as a step grid you click through rest, hit,
accent and ghost, plus a sound pick per voice with a play button that auditions the voice alone. A
bass or melody shows its note string with a piano roll under it that writes the string for you, a
seed stepper for the generated line when there is no written one, a patch pick, and the follow
checkbox. A pad shows its arpeggio. A sample part shows the file's waveform with the region and the
slice lines on it, drag to move a break, click to add one, a button per slice to hear it, and a
tempo guess with two buttons: write this part's bar count, or set the song's tempo. Every row ends
with a level slider, the volume control the axes deliberately lack, and a dB slot that the measure
button fills.

Every pick on the page opens the same menu: a filter box, and a row per option with a play button
that auditions it **without selecting it**. The kit menu plays every kit through the same two bars
of material, and names the voices a kit lacks.

## What you hear and what is written

Every control on the page falls on one side of this line:

**Solo, mute, A/B and the preview level change what you hear and never touch the file.**

Everything else, every knob and every menu and every dragged number, is a source edit that goes
through one undo stack.

<VizFigure
  name="Two paths out of one source file"
  summary="Controls that change the song write through a single commit function into one undo stack. The four controls that change only what is audible read an evaluated copy and never write back, so the file stays a clean record of intent."
>
  <PathsViz class="mx-auto h-auto w-full max-w-xl" aria-hidden="true" />
</VizFigure>

Solo is a question about a mix, not a decision about a song. "Is the pad doing anything?" should not
cost an edit, an undo, and a diff you have to remember to notice. Here it costs a click:

<SoundBite
  label="Two bars with everything, then the same two bars with one part alone"
  note="Soloing did not change the file. The isolated part is bit-identical to that part inside the song."
  clips={[
    { src: dropFull, label: "the whole section" },
    { src: soloPad, label: "the pad alone" },
  ]}
/>

That bit-identical claim comes out of the structure. The function that arranges a
song's sections into one pattern takes an optional filter, and solo and mute are that function
handed a filter. There is no second mixer. Because the solo path goes through the same arranger, the
mixer **cannot** drift from the renderer. It is the renderer.

The file also stays a clean record of intent, which is what an agent editing it reads. A mixer that
wrote solo state into the source would be quietly lying to the next request.

Two more things bypass the stack, and they are about where you are rather than what you hear:
pinning a section and scrubbing the playhead. Nothing that changes the music escapes it.

<Callout variant="note" title="One undo stack">
  The code editor ships with its own undo history, and it is not wired up. With three
  editors on the page plus the knobs, the pills, the chord picker and the section operations, there
  would have been four separate undo stacks and no way to step back through a session. Typing and
  knob drags coalesce into one step per second; section operations and kit changes never coalesce.
</Callout>

## Why a drum voice previews alone

When you audition a single drum voice from the menu, it plays on its own rather than inside the
part. The comment says why: the whole part would drown it, or not even contain it.

That second clause is literal. Voices join a drum part at thresholds, and the clap's is 0.85:

broken.

## Words, and whether they worked

The phrase row under the materials is where the vocabulary lives on the page. Type a word and it
becomes a pill, but only if it is a word the language knows; anything else stays red and never
becomes one. A part's name is a pill too, and it scopes the words after it to that part. **Apply**
rewrites the numbers and reports what it did, saturation and refusals included.

**Verify** does something a text file cannot. It renders the section as it stands, applies the words,
renders it again, measures both with the same analyser the command line uses, and shows a table:
one row per axis you asked for, its metric, the before and after values, and whether the number
moved the way you asked. Then it applies the edit, puts both renders on a pair of play buttons, and
you listen.

The verdict is a sign test, one row per axis, and it is deliberately not a stronger claim than that.
The word `punchier` moves four axes at once:

```text
Requested:  articulation +0.40, weight +0.15, drive +0.20, space -0.15
Render:     crest 5.11 -> 4.27   lowRatio 0.518 -> 0.482   centroidHz 573 -> 449
Result:     articulation NOT verified (crest 5.11 -> 4.27), weight NOT verified,
            drive code-checked: onset count unchanged (15/cyc), positions not measured,
            space verified (tail 0.022 -> 0.016)
```

Articulation is judged by crest factor. Drive re-places where the onsets fall. So drive moved the
measurement that articulation was being scored on, and articulation reads as a failure. Listen to it.
It is unmistakably punchier:

<SoundBite
  label="The request the tool refused to confirm"
  note="Two bars of drums, before and after the word punchier. Measured 2026-09-13 with the command-line verify."
  clips={[
    { src: punchierBefore, label: "before" },
    { src: punchierAfter, label: "after: punchier" },
  ]}
/>

A tool that said "verified" there would be more pleasant and less useful. Four axes moved, three of
them cannot be separated by these metrics, and the strongest available claim is about one of them.
"Verified directionally" is the ceiling written into the project's own instructions, and this is what
enforcing it costs. Two caveats: the page's Verify measures the whole section
mix and applies a plain sign test, while the command-line version renders one part alone and adds a
two percent floor, so the same request can score differently in the two places.

The same offline renderer is behind the **measure** button, which renders the section and then each
part alone and writes, next to every level slider, how many decibels under the mix that part sits.
That turns "is the choir too loud?" into a number, and [the mixing post](/mixing-as-a-scene/) is
about what to do with it.

## Notes you pin while listening become the next request

Press play. The transport position follows the clock, so wherever the slider is *is* where your ear
is. When something bothers you, type a sentence and press a button. The comment pins at that exact
cycle, tagged with the section it fell in. Drag the dot to re-pin it, because the ear reacts a moment
late and "actually it was a beat earlier" should be a drag rather than a retype.

Then one button assembles a request: the section map with bar counts, the numbered notes rendered as
`cycle 26.5 (will, bar 2 of 8): the piano lost its sustain`, and the full source of every section
anyone pinned.

That works because of a rule in the request builder:

```js
// A note in a section's first bar also quotes the section before it: the ear often reacts a moment late.
```

A note landing in the first bar of a section quotes the *previous* section's source too. Because what
bothered you probably started before you noticed it.

## What the public site cannot do

The same page is deployed on GitHub Pages with no server behind it, and four things there need the
local one, because they touch disk: saving to the song file, the change comments card, importing a
sample file, and the Samples workshop page where a pack's named regions are defined. Saving goes to
the browser's local storage instead. And the page wants a desktop window: under about 60rem it shows
a warning row rather than hiding anything, because the Mix card does not fit.

Everything else above still works online, because it all runs in the browser anyway: play, the Mix
card, Verify, measure, the MP3 export (rendered offline in the browser, one cycle at a time, and
encoded there too), the Strudel export, and the Link button, which packs the whole song into the URL
with the browser's own compression API so nobody needs a paste bin.
