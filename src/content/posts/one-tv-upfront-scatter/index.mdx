AOL bought PrecisionDemand in May 2014, and I spent the four years after that on one product. First
as the senior engineer leading the migration off Silverlight and onto AngularJS, then, from June
2016, as a Principal running a UI team of three and owning everything a user saw on the Linear TV
advertising platform. The platform was called ONE TV.

Two directories in [my 2018 portfolio](/labs-2018-snapshot/) hold what is left of it: `upfront` and
`scatter`. Between them they contain shipped screens, a hand-annotated wireframe, and — hiding in
the scatter folder — the same dialog drawn three times across five years.

<Callout variant="note" title="The campaigns are not real">
  Every advertiser, budget and rating number in these screenshots is demo data. Verizon, YSL's Black
  Opium, the Nielsen figures — all of it was built for demos.
</Callout>

## What I remember, and what I don't

Upfront and scatter are the two halves of how television inventory gets bought. I cannot tell you
how they split ONE TV as a product. Whether they were two products, two modes of one product, or
just two ways the same screens got used, is not something I still carry around eight years later,
and I would rather say that than reconstruct a strategy from thumbnails.

So this post does the thing I can actually do honestly: it describes the screens, and it follows
one dialog through three generations of them.

## Upfront: a quarter, planned all at once

The shipped upfront screen is a scenario comparison. You pick a campaign, a date range, a network
group and a demographic, and then you make as many plans as you like against that one budget and
put them side by side.

Everything about that screenshot is ordinary except how much of it is people. The campaign is
"Verizon Q2 2018" and the quarter runs 26 March to 25 June — a whole quarter of inventory, bought
in one sitting, against the Univision network group and its GALA, UMA and UNI networks. Another
screen in the same folder shows an NBCU list instead: BRAV, CNBC, E!, MSNB, NBC, SYFY, USA. But the
scenario names in the grid are `Sara 1`, `Olga- shift regress`, `Test 363`, `Decrease Prod Redo`.
That is not a product demo. That is Sara and Olga poking at the optimizer on a Tuesday, and I
apparently never cleared it out before taking the screenshot.

Each scenario runs the optimizer with a different objective — shift the buy, decrease the budget —
and the grid ranks the results by what they cost and how well they hit the target. By 2018 there were
three scoring columns on that grid — MVS, MQS, SMC — three ways of scoring the same plan. I could
not tell you today what all three of those initialisms stood for.

## Scatter: the tactical half

Scatter is the other half of the year. The upfront market sells a quarter or a season in advance;
scatter is the tactical buying that happens inside the quarter, once budgets have moved and there
is inventory left over to place against.

a ranking of two completely different pieces of inventory on how efficiently they reach the people
you are paying to reach.

## The same dialog, three generations deep

Balsamiq first, then code. That was how I worked at PrecisionDemand and it was still how I worked
at AOL: draw the whole screen, annotate the behaviour on it in callouts, argue about the drawing,
then build the drawing.

The callouts are the specification. "Creating a scenario automatically adds a new column to the grid
below (and a new series in all charting)." "All charts show ALL scenarios AND all update on every
change." "Ready to print for sending to network" — because the end of all this optimizing was a
piece of paper that told a network which spots to buy and which to sell back.

The wireframe is a Q3 campaign against FOX Networks Group at \$1.25M, 21% of the total. The shipped
screen is a Q2 campaign against Univision at \$2.65M. Different quarter, different networks, same
screen.

The scatter folder did not get cleaned out either: alongside the 2018 product it still holds the
drawings and dialogs it grew out of, sitting in the same directory listing as if no time had
passed. Go back to the start of that folder.

Minimum cluster score, minimum blended score, maximum single buy percentage, maximum single
count, minimum CPM per unit. Five years, one rename: what had been a **program** became an
**airing**, which is a more precise word for the same thing. The numbers in the boxes did move — the
2013 build shipped stricter score floors than the drawing asked for and a far looser cap on any
single buy, which is what happens when a design meets real inventory. But the fields, and their
order, are the fields.

I do not think anyone decided this. Nobody wrote down a design language and made us follow it. The
vocabulary was just load-bearing enough that every rewrite carried it forward rather than paying to
re-derive it — and by 2017 I was drawing wireframes against a list of constraints I had first drawn
in 2012, mostly out of muscle memory.

## What happened to it

AOL renamed the division Oath in June 2017 — same team, same platform, a new logo on the same
screens. I left in March 2018, and the reason these screenshots exist at all is that I was taking
them on the way out, for a portfolio. ONE TV was shuttered after I left.

So the product is gone, and what survived it is a directory listing where a 2012 drawing, a 2013
dialog and a 2018 web app all sit next to each other, and you cannot tell from the field labels
which is which.
