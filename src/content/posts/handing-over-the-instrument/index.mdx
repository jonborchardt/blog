The three posts before this one built a thing: [a system nobody read](/never-read-the-code/), [a
language over Strudel](/a-language-on-top-of-strudel/), and [a vocabulary that edits it](/say-darker/).
All of it was built for a person to use.

This post is about handing it to something that cannot hear.

## The agent cannot hear, and it cannot render either

The second half of that is the part I keep having to restate. There is a chain that turns "brighter"
into a measured number: render the section before, apply the edit, render it after, analyse both,
compare. It works. But the rendering happens **in a browser**, because the audio engine is Web Audio.
Node never makes a sound.

So the setup is: a headless Chromium sits there with the page open, and the command-line tools ask it
to render things over a local socket. With no browser open, the agent has no ears *and* no
instrument. It falls back to reading the event stream.

What stands in for ears, in order of how often each gets used:

1. **The event dump.** Evaluate the file in Node, query every event in the song. For the 59-bar piece
   that is 2,751 events. Any sound name the packs do not have fails the build.
2. **The sounds block**, which prints the actual file behind every sound name. This exists because of
   a bug the last post in this series gets to.
3. **The section table and the energy arc**, which answer "what is the shape of this" without audio.
4. **The axis values read back as words**, with a deadband: a move smaller than 0.15 produces no word
   at all.

## What "verified" actually means

  <VerifyViz class="mx-auto h-auto w-full max-w-2xl" aria-hidden="true" />

The verdict is a **sign test with a two percent floor**, on one scalar, over one part of one section
rendered on its own. Not a calibrated measurement: depending on how dense the part is, the onset
detector under-counts the known event count by anywhere from half to three quarters, and still gets
the direction right every time. Ordinal, not absolute.

Across seven runs it produced fifteen verdicts: **seven verified, five not verified, two
code-checked, one not applicable.** Every request that moved a single measurable axis verified. The
misses came from two causes.

Here is the miss. The word was "punchier", which moves four axes at once:

```text
Requested:  articulation +0.40, weight +0.15, drive +0.20, space -0.15
Render:     crest 5.11 -> 4.27   lowRatio 0.518 -> 0.482   centroidHz 573 -> 449
Result:     articulation NOT verified (crest 5.11 -> 4.27), weight NOT verified,
            drive code-checked: onset count unchanged (15/cyc), positions not measured,
            space verified (tail 0.022 -> 0.016)
```

Articulation is judged by crest factor. Drive re-places where the onsets fall. So drive moved the
measurement that articulation was being scored on, and articulation reads as a failure.

Listen to it. It is unmistakably punchier:

<Callout variant="note" title="Why I like this failure">
  A tool that said "verified" there would be more pleasant and less useful. The honest reading is
  that four axes moved, three of them cannot be separated by these metrics, and the strongest
  available claim is about one of them. "Verified directionally" is the ceiling written into the
  agent's own instructions, and this is what enforcing it costs.
</Callout>

The second cause is the one [the previous post](/say-darker/) is entirely about. Ask for "much
brighter" on a part whose filter is already open past anything the sample contains, and the value
saturates: the cutoff went from 3482 Hz to 8000 Hz and the measured centroid moved *down* by six
hertz. Nothing was wrong with the request or the edit. There was simply no brightness left to add,
and the metric reported the truth.

The structural limitation is bigger than either. Verification renders **one part of one section,
alone**. Masking, headroom, anything that only exists in the sum: invisible. Hold that thought.

## The first pass is not the problem

The claim I went in with was that the first generated version is always competent and generic, and
everything good comes from round two onward. Half of that survived contact with the evidence, and it
is the less flattering half for me rather than for the agent.

**Generic in material: yes, and it is measurable.** The industrial track's first pass drew 24 lint
warnings. Sixteen of them said some part "plays the seeded line: write its notes in mini-notation",
which is to say every melody and bass in the song was a dice roll. Eight said two raw sawtooths in one
section are mud. Every pitched part was a bare sawtooth.

**Generic in form: no, not remotely.** Twelve sections with roles, an arc written into the header
comment, twelve bespoke hand-written texture patterns, and an ending that burns out rather than
resolving. The first pass was ambitious and unspecific, which is not the same thing as generic.

And then the orchestral piece inverts the claim completely. Eight recorded requests, seven of them
feedback, over a two-hour session, and its energy arc after all of it is **unchanged on seven of its
eight sections** and moved by three tenths of a point on the last. Same eight sections, same shape,
one line longer.

Almost every round was about sound rather than music: a sample index, a reverb size, a delay send,
whether vibrato belongs on a sampled instrument. Two rounds asked for a section to be shorter, and
one asked for an architectural change. None asked for different notes.

So the honest version is:

> The first pass gets the shape right and the sound wrong. What later rounds buy is not better ideas.
> It is the removal of the things only a listener can detect.

## What a round of feedback looks like

The industrial track took five rounds in sixteen minutes. The commit gaps are the interesting part:
**eleven minutes, then two, one, one, one.**

At the time, that song ran three minutes twelve. So the eleven-minute gap is one full listen plus writing the notes,
and the one-minute gaps are someone re-listening to a single section and saying one sentence. The
shape of the log is the shape of the advice.

### Correcting a wrong guess

The human said they did not like the horn. The agent removed a brown-noise swell. The human said the
horn was still there.

Two minutes later, one commit does exactly two things: it deletes the melody part from both verses,
and it puts the noise swell back. That diff is the entire argument for blunt correction. Not "let me
re-explain what I meant", just "you removed the wrong thing", and the round costs two minutes.

## The five words that became a linter

The most consequential piece of feedback in the whole repository is a sentence: **"the music sounds
flat and simplistic."** It appears verbatim as the recorded ask in four songs' notes files, one of which has since been deleted.

What it produced was not just an edit. It produced a commit that touched the layer library, four
songs, four provenance files and the test fixtures. Then it propagated upward into the agent's own
instructions, which grew a section titled *"Flat, thin, simplistic, boring, aimless: material first,
not axes"*:

> Those words mean the notes and the sounds, and no axis fixes them. Adding more layers at once adds
> mass, not interest.

And later it turned into static analysis: a linter rule that flags any melody or bass left on its
generated default.

Ear, then a written rule, then a rule a script can check. One human sentence, turned into something
that can be asked mechanically rather than remembered. That is the loop I would want from any of
this, and it is worth saying that the last step stopped one short: as
[the first post](/never-read-the-code/) notes, that linter never made it into continuous integration,
so it is a check you have to choose to run.

## Editing by comment

Which brings me to the mechanism I like best in the whole system, because it is barely a mechanism.

Press play. The transport position follows the clock, so wherever the slider is *is* where your ear
is. When something bothers you, type a sentence and press a button. The comment pins at that exact
cycle, to one decimal, tagged with the section it fell in. Drag the dot to re-pin it, because the ear
reacts a moment late and "actually it was a beat earlier" should be a drag rather than a retype.

Then one button assembles a request: the section map with bar counts, the numbered notes rendered as
`cycle 22.4 (will, bar 2 of 8): the piano has no sustain here`, and the full source of every section
anyone pinned.

The rule I did not expect, and the one that shows somebody thought about it:

```js
// A note in a section's first bar also quotes the section before it: the ear often reacts a moment late.
```

A note landing in the first bar of a section quotes the *previous* section's source too. Because what
bothered you probably started before you noticed it.

## Provenance, and what the record actually looks like

Every request is supposed to be recorded: the ask in the human's words, then per change a section, a
part, an axis, a from and a to, and a why in musical terms.

That is the schema. The data is more interesting. Across every song: **43 recorded changes, of which
3 carry a from and a to, 23 name a part, and exactly one names an axis.**

The structured fields went unused because most real changes were not axis moves. They were a sample
index, a sound name, a texture, a bar count. Material has no from-and-to. The provenance schema was
designed for the axis system, and the axis system turned out not to be where the work happened.

What survived is the `why` field, and it carries things nothing else in the repository knows:

> reverb size .9 on every event: superdough keeps one reverb per orbit and rebuilds its impulse
> response whenever an event arrives with a different size, and the pads and beds carried four sizes;
> the rebuild swapped the reverb buffer under every piano strike and at the section edge, **which no
> soloed part reproduces because alone each part has one size**

Read that last clause again, then remember what verification does: it renders one part, alone. This
is a bug that is structurally invisible to the entire measurement chain. It exists only in the sum,
and nothing in the tooling could have found it.

It is also not the end of that story. The same session contains a *second* artefact that took four
rounds and four wrong answers, and the reverb finding above turned out to be correct
about something else entirely. That belongs to the last post, because it belongs to one piece of
music.

## What I would tell someone starting this

- Give feedback one line per section, all in one message, after one listen.
- Say what you love. It tells the agent what to keep and what to reuse.
- Correct a wrong guess bluntly and immediately. It costs two minutes.
- Ask for "more" in small steps. Each round should add one idea.
- Expect the first pass to have the right shape and the wrong sound.

And the one that took me longest: **the human is the only sensor in the system.** Everything else is
instrumentation, and instrumentation is a thing you read, not a thing that hears.
