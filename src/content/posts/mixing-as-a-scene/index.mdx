Measured before, the loudest thing in that climax was the impact hit, five decibels under the whole
mix. The choir was a decibel louder than the piano theme it was supposed to sit behind, and shared
the midrange with the organ. The timpani pulse was thirty decibels under the mix and the
glockenspiel doubling was forty, which is to say neither could be heard at all. Nothing had a
position, and the parts were spread over five separate reverbs.

This post is about what "give every part a place" means when the tool has no faders, only a
language: the handful of keys strudel-bench added for mixing, the perception each one leans on, and
the measurement that says whether it worked. The earlier posts covered [the language](/a-language-on-top-of-strudel/),
[the words](/say-darker/) and [the editor](/knobs-in-the-code/); this one is about the newest and
least finished part of the system.

## What a mix is, if it is not volume

A mixing engineer's first move is not the fader. It is deciding where each thing is: how far
forward, how far left, how much of the room it sits in, and which two things are fighting for the
same air. Volume falls out of those decisions.

Perception gives you a few handles for "where":

- **Level** tells you how far away, but only roughly, because a quiet close sound and a loud distant
  one can arrive at the same loudness.
- **Brightness** tells you distance more reliably: air and rooms swallow high frequencies, so a dull
  version of a sound reads as farther away than a bright one at the same level.
- **The reverb tail**, and how the direct sound sits in front of it, tells you the size of the room and
  where in it the source is. If the first reflections arrive a few tens of milliseconds after the
  direct sound, the ear localises the direct sound and folds the reflections into it. That is the
  precedence effect, and it is why a reverb with a short silent head in front of it keeps a part in
  place while a reverb with none smears it.
- **Masking**: a louder sound at a nearby frequency raises the threshold at which a quieter one can be
  heard at all. Two parts in the same band at the same time do not add; one of them disappears.
- **Timing**: a line whose notes land exactly on the grid reads as a machine. One whose notes lean
  late and early together, in a shape that repeats, reads as played.

strudel-bench encodes each of those as a key you can write in a part or a section. None of it is a
fader. Here they are.

## One room

Before the pass, arrival's parts were in five different reverbs, because each kind of part sets its
own reverb size from its `space` axis, and superdough keeps one reverb per bus. Put two parts that
want different reverbs on one bus and the engine rebuilds the impulse response under whichever note
arrives next. The piece's own notes record that happening under the piano, and blame it for the
piano's pop.

The fix is a `room` on the song:

```js
song({ bpm: 64, key: 'D:minor', seed: 41, kit: 'RolandTR808', packs: ['rooms'],
       room: { ir: 'hall', size: 3.2, damping: 4500 } }, [
```

With a room set, every part in the section shares one convolver, and each part's `space` value stops
meaning "which reverb" and becomes "how much of it": its send. Thirteen parts, one hall, each with
its own amount of it.

The `ir` is a generated impulse response, not a recording. A small script builds a shoebox room from
its dimensions and absorption, places the mirror-image reflections as taps, then appends a seeded
noise tail that darkens as it decays. Three ship with the repo: a small room with an 8 ms silent
head, a plate with 15 ms, and a hall with 30 ms and a 3.2 second decay. The silent head is the
pre-delay, and it is baked into the file because the engine has no pre-delay control of its own; a
song can shorten it by starting the impulse partway in.

reverbs with no positions, then under one hall with a position each.

The perceptual claim is a modest one. Sources that share a room signature are grouped as being in
the same place; sources that each carry a different one read as a collage. Mixing engineers have
worked that way for decades, on one main reverb. Here it is one key.

## A position, and then a width

`position` runs from -1 (left) to 1 (right) and shifts every note's pan by half of it. It is
deliberately separate from `width`, which the language already had. Width is how much of the field a
part occupies; on most parts it is a slow pan sweep, on wide pads a mirrored copy in the other ear.
Position is where the centre of that is. Set both and the sweep moves around the position, and a
drum kit's per-voice spread moves as one block.

The measurement taught me two things about this. The rendered pan of a wet part sits near the centre
whatever its position says, because the hall's stereo return dominates its energy: the bowed
vibraphone is written at -.35, its notes carry that pan, and the measured stem reads as almost
centred. And the piano sample is right-heavy on its own, reading at .74 with no position at all. What
the ear gets is the position plus the sample plus the room, and the number in the file is only the
first of those.

The low end stays centred. Localisation below about a hundred hertz is poor, stereo bass wastes
headroom, and it falls apart on a mono speaker, so the measurement's flat-stage rule exempts the
drums and the bass from ever needing a position.

## Depth, in words

Depth is the axis the fader cannot do alone. The vocabulary got four words for it: `closer` and
`forward` raise brightness, lower space and add a little weight; `farther` and `background` do the
reverse. They move three axes at once, gently, because that is what distance does to a sound: it gets
duller, wetter and thinner together. The measurement's depth number is built from the same three
things, level, high-frequency share and reverb tail, and the tool insists you read it with its three
inputs, because a high depth from level alone is a quiet part, not a distant one.

The choir is the worked example. Before the pass it was as loud as the piano theme and sharing the
midrange with the organ. After: level cut, `farther` applied, brightness down from .6 to .4, sent
fully into the hall, and placed behind and to the right.

The level cut is not what moved the masking. The organ and choir shared the mids at .72 on the
measurement's scale before, and .45 after, comfortably under its .6 warning line. **The ten decibel
level cut did nothing to that score**, because the masking measure is level-independent by
construction: it asks where two parts' energy sits and whether they sound at the same time, not how
loud either is. What moved it was the choir getting darker. Its centroid fell from 1376 Hz to 877
Hz, out of the organ's band. A quieter part in the same band is still in the way; a darker one is
not.

## Levels by role, not by feel

Once every part has a place, the fader is the last thing you touch, and the project writes down what
it should do. Name each part's role and give it a band under the mix: a foreground part six to
twelve decibels under, support ten to eighteen, background sixteen to twenty-six, with the kick and
bass as foundation. Below thirty is inaudible: cut it or raise it, never leave it. These are the
author's defaults, marked as heuristics in the code, not laws of hearing.

The timpani pulse was thirty decibels under the mix at a level of 1, and the top of the level range,
2, only brought it to twenty-one. The sample is quiet. Meanwhile, by the score's own per-event gain,
the triangle in that same pulse is the loudest voice in the finale by a wide margin. The number the
language exposes and the number the render reports disagree, and only one of them is what you hear.

The doubling voice came up twenty-seven decibels by having its level **lowered**, from .45 to .2,
because it stopped being a glockenspiel and became a vibraphone. The glockenspiel sample was too
quiet to place at any level; a vibraphone at the same register is loud. Levels are per sample, not
per intention, and no amount of reasoning about the file would have found that. A render did.

## Making room: the sidechain

When a bass and a kick want the same air, you can dip the bass for the instant the kick hits and let
it recover. The transient gets through, the sustain comes back, and the two never sound at the same
moment in the band that matters.

```js
bass: { ...sub, duck: 'drums', duckDepth: .5, duckAttack: .12 },
pad:  { ...bed, duck: 'drums', duckDepth: .6, duckAttack: .25 },
```

`duck` names another part in the section. Its hits are pointed at this part's bus as a sidechain,
the depth sets how far the dip goes, and the recovery time sets whether it breathes or pumps. It is
superdough's own ducking, wired up by name. Before that existed the pad layer faked it, dipping a
hard-driven pad's gain once per chord, and the fake is still there as the fallback when no source is
named.

Arrival ducks nothing. A sixty-four beat-per-minute score with a timpani pulse has no kick to make
room for. The industrial track in the same repository ducks its bass and its sub pad, and still fails
the tool's headroom check, because its kit is distorted and the distortion happens after the gain:
a distorted part peaks at full scale whatever its level says. There is no master limiter, and the
tool says so.

## A played feel, not dice

The language has always had an `organicness` axis: above its midpoint it nudges every hit by a fresh
random amount and scales its gain by another. It sounds like what it is. Independent per-hit jitter
does not sound like a person; it sounds like a machine imitating one, because a player's timing is
not noise. Neighbouring notes lean together. The lean is different at different points in the bar. And
it is broadly the same on the next play, because it comes from the player's body and the phrase, not
from a coin.

`humanize` is the replacement, and it is material rather than an axis:

```js
humanize: { timingMs: 12, velocity: .1, correlation: 'phrase' }
```

Three seeded curves, evaluated at each note's time: a slow wave over the correlation span (a bar, a
phrase, or a count of bars), a fixed lean for each beat position inside the bar, and a small residual.
Their sum moves timing, gain and note length together, and because it is seeded it is the same on
every play and in every render.

One more key rounds out the set: a `compressor` per part, for the rare part that is spiky enough to
need it. The tubular bells strike twice a bar with a crest factor over nine, so they got one at a
threshold of -18 dB and a ratio of 3. The engine builds a compressor node per hit, so the linter
warns if you put one on anything dense.

## Measuring whether it worked

None of the above would be worth much if the only judge were my ear on my speakers. The measure
command renders a section's mix, then renders each part alone, and prints a table: each part's level
under the mix, its depth with the three inputs beside it, its measured pan, its peak, and then the
masking pairs, every pair of parts that shares a band at the same time, sorted by how badly. A second
linter reads that file and applies rules with written thresholds: no headroom, an inaudible part, a
dominant part, a flat stage where three or more parts all sit centre, two parts sharing the low band
or the mids past a line, no depth contrast.

Before the pass, arrival's climax drew five warnings: two inaudible parts and three pairs sharing the
mids. After it: clean. The mix's peak fell from .92 to .39, because the impact had been the peak, and
nothing clipped in either version.

<Callout variant="note" title="What clean does not mean">
  Two of the three masking pairs that were over the line, the choir and the organ each against the
  bowed vibraphone, now sit at .59 and .58 against a warning line of .6. The recorded note says so
  plainly: "left, as the register already separates them". Clean means under the lines, and the
  lines are heuristics from the first songs that were measured. The code marks every one of them as
  something to retune from renders, not by argument.
</Callout>

Every number above is a proxy. The masking score is three fixed
bands and a co-occurrence count, not a critical-band model; it is symmetric and level-independent,
where real masking is neither. The level column is unweighted RMS, not loudness, so a low pulse at
twenty-one decibels under is perceptually further down than a chime would be at the same number.
Depth is a weighted sum with hand-picked weights. Pan reads the rendered stem, room included. There is
no per-part EQ beyond one tilt, no true pre-delay control, no master bus. It measures four bars of a
section by default.

What it does do is turn a mix into a described object: a table of where things are and what is in
the way, with a verdict per rule. That is a thing a person can argue with, and it is a thing a program
that cannot hear can act on. The whole of [the piece it was built on](/arrival/) got its second draft
that way, and what the pass missed, it missed because it was not in the section that was measured. A
tool looks where it is pointed.
