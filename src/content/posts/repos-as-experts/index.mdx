[Part 1](/an-agent-built-this-blog/) explained why an agent can't ship a broken page here, and
[part 2](/the-authoring-surface/) showed the vocabulary posts are written in. This part is about
the harder problem: not *can the page build*, but *is the post any good — and is it true?* An
agent writing about a codebase it half-remembers will produce plausible, wrong prose all day.
This site's answer has two parts: the editorial process is a set of executable files, and every
technical post names another repository as its domain expert — a repo that gets consulted, like a
colleague, about the post describing its own work.

## The pipeline is a set of files

Editorial quality here is not a habit; it is seven procedure files, committed under
`.claude/skills/`, that an agent invokes and follows. Habits do not survive workers who forget
everything between sessions. Files do.

<VizFigure
  name="The editorial pipeline as skills"
  summary="write-post invokes create-visual and create-hero, then hands a draft to review-post; findings loop back to write-post, and a clean review goes to publish-post — the only place a draft flips to published."
>
  <PipelineViz class="mx-auto h-auto w-full max-w-2xl" aria-hidden="true" />
</VizFigure>

Each skill guards something specific. `write-post` owns the shape of a draft and ends with a
mandatory visual pass — its own rule says a technical post typically yields two to four visuals,
and *"zero means the pass was skipped, not that nothing qualified."* `create-hero` makes the
author write the hero image's alt text **first** — if you cannot write the sentence, the idea is
not concrete enough to draw. `review-post` runs the automated gates plus a human-judgment
read-through and outputs a findings list rather than silently rewriting anything. And
`publish-post` is the only place `draft: false` ever gets set; it ends by verifying the live URL,
the listings, and the RSS feed on the deployed site, not by trusting the push.

Two supporting skills sit off the main chain: `create-series` (registry entry first, post
second — the build fails on an unregistered series) and `wrapup`, a whole-site audit for
batches, built around grep sweeps where every hit is either a contract violation or must
justify itself.

<Aside>
  The skills deliberately do not restate the rules in `CLAUDE.md` or the component README — they
  link to them. One source of truth per fact, for procedures as much as for colours.
</Aside>

## Every post names its expert

The frontmatter of every technical post here carries an `authority` field: the path to the
repository that owns that post's domain facts. For the three posts about upscaling Sierra
adventure-game backgrounds, that is the SCI rendering repo. For the retirement-simulation posts,
the simulator. For this series, the blog's own repo.

The word *authority* is doing real work. The contract — written into `write-post` and shared by
the other skills — says the authority repo owns "what the story actually is, what matters and
what is misleading": behavior, terminology, data meaning, technical claims, evidence, caveats,
and the semantics of every visual. The blog owns the artifact: structure, prose, MDX, visual
design, accessibility. The expert is a co-author of meaning, not a fact-checker of last resort.

Mechanically, an agent is dispatched *into* the authority repo, where it reads that repo's own
instructions, source, tests, and data, and makes domain decisions. The budget is strict — two
calls per post:

  name="The authority lifecycle"

>

</VizFigure>

The first call produces a **domain brief**: the strongest version of the story, the claims worth
making with their evidence, corrections to the author's outline, terminology, caveats, and — for
every proposed visual — the exact data and semantics needed to draw it correctly. The brief is
committed to the post's directory at `research/brief.md`, in public, and everything downstream
works from it instead of re-asking. The second call validates the finished draft against what the
expert previously asserted. A targeted extra question is allowed only when the brief genuinely
cannot answer it, and the answer gets appended to the brief.

<Callout variant="note" title="Why commit the brief?">
  A committed brief is public evidence a reader can check, a cache the next revision works from,
  and an anchor for review — the validator compares the draft against what the expert said, not
  against vibes. The `authority` path itself is just a machine address on the author's disk; the
  brief is the part of the expertise that ships.
</Callout>

## What an expert actually said

The system earns its keep when the expert pushes back. The first retrofitted brief on this site —
for the post on [AGI-UP as a reference renderer](/agi-up-as-a-reference-renderer/) — came back
with eight numbered factual corrections to an already-published post. It found the post's
pipeline diagram had two stages in the wrong order ("The repo says the opposite… the seal runs
*before* gap closure"). It found the ending confidently predicted a next step that never
happened, and named what actually happened instead. It flagged one of the author's own claims as
unverifiable and said to keep it only if the author personally vouches. It even caught the
authority repo contradicting *itself* — two configs, two different thresholds — and told the
blog to disclose the discrepancy rather than paper over it.

And asked to propose new visuals, it declined:

<Quote cite="the AGI-UP domain brief, on proposed visuals">
  None earn their place… the one dataset that would genuinely add depth… no longer exists in the
  repo.
</Quote>

An expert that says *no* is the strongest evidence the role is real.

## What the system does not guarantee

Honest limits, in order of sharpness. The expert can be wrong — its own repo can disagree with
itself, as the threshold discrepancy above shows; the contract buys an accountable, evidence-cited
process, not truth. Briefs go stale — the corrected post above had an ending that was true when
written and false months later; a brief is a snapshot with an append rule, not a live feed.
Nothing enforces brief *quality* — a lazy investigation would produce a thin brief and a green
build; the backstops are the validation pass and the fact that briefs are public. And coverage is
still partial: the authority system was adopted after ten technical posts were already live, and
a temporary retrofit skill is backfilling briefs onto them, tracking its own progress in a
committed worklist — three of ten through the expert pass as this publishes, and the skill's
final instruction is to delete itself when the list is done.

One design seam worth noticing: `authority` is optional in the frontmatter schema but required by
the writing skill. That split is deliberate. The Zod schema enforces what a machine can check —
lengths, registries, date logic. The skills enforce what only procedure can check. The build
gates from [part 1](/an-agent-built-this-blog/) keep an agent from shipping a broken page; the
process gates here keep it from shipping a fluent, well-formed, wrong one.

## This post, as evidence

This post's own `authority` is the blog repository. That is not a joke; it is the rule applied
uniformly — for a post about the pipeline, the pipeline's repo is the correct expert, the same
way the SCI renderer was for the upscaling posts. The investigation pass for this post ran like
any other: an agent was dispatched into the repo, read the seven skills and the one existing
retrofit brief, and returned the domain brief this post was then written from. It is committed at
`research/brief.md` in this post's directory, where you can check whether the expert and the
prose agree. When the authority and the artifact are the same repo, the division of labour is
procedural rather than physical — two passes and a committed brief instead of two codebases — but
the paper trail is the same.

That is the whole system, and this series is its output: contracts that make broken states
unbuildable, a vocabulary an agent can wield without inventing anything, and an editorial process
that treats repositories as colleagues with domain knowledge worth consulting. The receipts, as
always, are in the repo.
