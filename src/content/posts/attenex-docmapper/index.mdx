I graduated from the University of Washington's computer science program and went straight to a
small company in downtown Seattle that had decided lawyers should look at documents instead of
reading them. I was one of the first engineers there. I stayed six years, from August 2001 to
October 2007, and I spent nearly all of that time on one screen.

The screen was called the Document Mapper — DocMapper, to everyone who used it. It drew an entire
legal document review as a map: every document a dot, similar documents gathered into rings,
rings strung along lines of shared meaning, and the whole thing wrapped in a compass of the
concepts that organised it. It became the company's flagship product and its primary market
differentiator, and it left me with five granted patents. It is one of the three projects I am
proudest of in [the portfolio this series is walking through](/labs-2018-snapshot/).

## The pile

The domain was electronic discovery: when a matter goes to litigation, both sides have to hand
over the relevant documents, which in practice means someone has to look at all of them and decide
which ones are relevant. In 2001 "all of them" had just stopped meaning filing cabinets and
started meaning mail servers. The pile was suddenly measured in millions of items, and the review
method had not changed at all. It was still one document, one human, one decision, over and over,
in linear order, by people billing hourly.

The company's answer was a platform called Attenex Patterns. Patterns pulled the text out of
everything, ran it through linguistic analysis to extract **concepts** — the reviewer guide defines
one as "a noun or noun phrase in the text of a document" — and gave each document a concept
profile. The guide has a nice line about it: think of the profile as "an extended 'subject line'
describing its content." The language machinery underneath was mostly other people's — the front
matter of the guide credits Inxight's LinguistX Platform for the linguistic analysis and dtSearch
for full-text search, and carries the copyright notice for Chih-Chung Chang and Chih-Jen Lin's
support vector machine library.

DocMapper was the front end for that. All the analysis in the world does not help if the reviewer
still has to open documents one at a time, and the map was the argument that they should not have
to.

Every dot is one document, and its colour is the category a reviewer has marked it with. Documents
whose concept profiles look alike are gathered into a **cluster**, drawn with a ring around it. A
cluster is not a folder anyone made; the guide is careful to call it "a temporary grouping of
documents based on the similarity of their concepts", and it dissolves and re-forms every time you
recluster.

Clusters that share a concept are joined by a line called a **spine** — "a line of clusters that
share a common concept" — and a cluster sitting where two spines cross belongs to both. Hovering a
cluster thickened its spine so you could see, immediately, what else was on the same thread.

Around the outside sits the **concept compass**: the concepts of the longest spines, positioned so
that each label points along the spine it names. That ring is the thing I would defend hardest if
someone asked me what the design was for. You could not read a million documents, but you could
read a handful of words arranged around the edge of a circle, and those words told you what the
collection was about before you had clicked anything.

The layout inside a cluster was not decoration either. Documents were "arranged in a spiral, based
on their similarity to the center document", so proximity meant something at every zoom level: the
compass told you what the collection was about, the spine told you what a neighbourhood was about,
the ring told you what a group was about, and the position of a dot inside the ring told you how
typical that document was of its group.

Down the right-hand side were the **set-aside trays**. Once you had decided about a document, it
left the map and went into a tray, so the main region always showed you only the work you had not
done yet.

A few things refused to be mapped, and DocMapper was honest about them rather than hiding them.
Documents with no extractable concepts at all — images, password-protected files — went into a
square called **Zero Concepts**. Short documents with too few useful concepts went into **Limited
Concepts**. Long documents with plenty of concepts that were simply not like anything else went
into **Miscellaneous Files**. In the screenshot above, that is 86 documents out of the assignment
that the clustering declined to have an opinion about. I still think putting them on screen, in
their own labelled squares, was the right call. A visualization that quietly drops what it cannot
explain is lying.

## Where the tenfold came from

The claim we made, and the one I still repeat, is that this was roughly ten times faster than
traditional review. It is worth being precise about where that came from, because it was not
rendering and it was not machine learning. It came from changing the unit of work.

Traditional review is one document, one decision. DocMapper's review is one *cluster*, one
decision. Select a ring, glance at the top concepts that pulled it together, mark the whole ring
Non-responsive, sweep it into a tray, recluster, and the map redraws around what is left.
Repeat until the only things still on screen are the documents that genuinely need a human to read
them.

The reviewer guide put this better than any of our marketing did, and I am quoting it here because
it is the clearest statement of the whole product I have ever found:

<Quote cite="Attenex Patterns Document Mapper Reviewer Guide, version 3.5">
  Reviewers who are wanting to eliminate large numbers of non-responsive documents from an
  assignment may decide to not look for the proverbial "needle in a haystack", but instead look for
  entire "haystacks with no needles".
</Quote>

The guide goes on to say that if a reviewer cannot make a decision about a cluster within a few
seconds of looking at its concepts, they should leave it unmarked and move on. That instruction is
the tenfold, written down. The map's job was to make the easy majority of the pile obviously easy,
fast, so that the expensive human attention landed only on the residue.

There was a second half to it that I liked even more. The reviewer could throw concepts *away*.
Days of the week, phone numbers, the boilerplate at the bottom of everyone's signature — a concept
that was structuring the map without meaning anything could be dragged off the compass and dropped
into a trash bin in the corner, and the next reclustering would be computed as though it had never
existed. The clustering was not an oracle handing down an answer. It was a knob, and the person who
knew the case got to turn it.

<Callout variant="note" title="On the word 3D">
  The patents and my own résumé call this a dense three-dimensional scene, and that is what we built
  it on: a real graphics pipeline, with depth. But look at the screenshots and you will see a plane
  you pan and zoom, not a scene you fly through. The dimensionality was in the engine and in how
  densely we could push pixels, more than in what the reviewer experienced. I would rather say that
  plainly than let the word do work it did not do.
</Callout>

## Building it

The first version was raw OpenGL. Not a scene graph, not a framework — an assignment is more
primitives than an early-2000s middle layer wanted to hear about, and all of them had to hit the
screen at an interactive frame rate on whatever workstation a law firm happened to own, which meant
owning the pipeline myself. The client was C++ and MFC, with SQL underneath. Later I led the
migration from OpenGL to DirectX, which bought us both performance and better-looking output, and
the codebase drifted toward C# as the years went on.

Most of the six years were performance work, honestly. Not the fun kind of graphics performance
either — the kind where a reviewer opens an assignment of a size nobody tested and you spend a week
in a database profiler. The résumé line for that period is "profiled and tuned application and
database performance", which is a tidy way of saying that the difference between a tool people love
and a tool people are forced to use is usually a few hundred milliseconds wide.

In July 2006 I moved from Software Engineer to Senior, and the job changed shape: I owned the
DocMapper client outright — architecture, features, performance, quality — specified features
end to end, and started mentoring other engineers on visualization. When I left in October 2007 my
VP of engineering wrote a reference that I have kept ever since:

<Quote cite="Skip Walter, VP Engineering, Attenex">
  Jon has matured during his six years evolving from a competent programmer to a senior software
  engineer... I highly recommend Jon for senior software engineering positions.
</Quote>

## Three more ways to look at the same pile

The cluster view was the centrepiece, but a document collection has more than one shape, and the
product eventually grew a view for each of them. All of them shared highlighting: search or select
in one, and the matching documents lit up yellow in all the others at once.

**Bird's Eye** was the small always-whole-map inset, with a rectangle marking your current
viewport. Click anywhere in it to jump. Unglamorous, and the piece I would keep if I could only
keep one.

**Timeline** laid the same documents out by date — sent, received, or last modified. Drag across a
range of months and those documents highlight everywhere else. It is the view that answers "what
happened in May", which in litigation is very often the actual question.

  <Image

**Social Network View** drew the participants instead of the documents: every sender and recipient
a circle sized by volume, every pair who exchanged mail joined by a line — grey for one-way, green
for two-way. It had three searches I remember with real fondness, because they encode a piece of
domain knowledge directly into the UI. *Find Zero Received* highlights people who send but never
receive, which is mostly automated mail. *Find Zero Sent* highlights people who only ever receive,
which is mostly distribution lists. *Find Single Exchanges* highlights the one-off blasts copied to
everybody. One menu, and each item on it deletes a large category of noise from a review.

One more piece of the model deserves a mention because it took real arguing to get right. An email
with two attachments is not one document. Patterns split it into three, so the body and each
attachment clustered on their own concepts and landed wherever they actually belonged — which was
often three different places on the map. But you cannot *produce* half an email in litigation, so
DocMapper computed a separate **production mark** for the compound item from the marks on its
components, and that is what governed the export. The reviewer worked at the level that made sense
for reading; the export worked at the level that made sense for the law.

## Five patents

Five granted patents came out of that work, all of them about the same underlying problem: how a
user interface stays usable when the scene is far too dense for the screen it is on.

| Patent        | Title                                                                                                                          |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| US 7,356,777  | System and method for providing a dynamic user interface for a dense three-dimensional scene                                   |
| US 7,404,151  | System and method for providing a dynamic user interface for a dense three-dimensional scene                                   |
| US 7,870,507  | System and method for providing a dynamic user interface for a dense three-dimensional scene with a navigation assistance panel |
| US 8,056,019  | System and method for providing a dynamic user interface including a plurality of logical layers                               |
| US 8,402,395  | System and method for providing a dynamic user interface for a dense three-dimensional scene with a plurality of compasses      |

Read the titles in order and you can watch the product being invented. The dense scene comes first.
Then the navigation assistance panel, which is Bird's Eye. Then logical layers, which is how labels,
rings, spines and dots could all occupy the same pixels without turning into mud. Then a plurality
of compasses, which is what happens when one ring of concepts is not enough.

<Callout variant="note" title="A caveat about the dates">
  My own patent records list "issued" dates for these that cannot be right — several are years
  earlier than a patent with that number could have been granted, so they are almost certainly
  filing dates that got mislabelled somewhere along the way. Rather than repeat a date I cannot
  vouch for, I have given numbers and titles only.
</Callout>

I do not want to oversell my part in these. Patents are drafted by attorneys and typically carry
several inventors; what I did was author and edit the disclosures for interfaces I had built. But
the ideas in them are the ideas in the screenshots above, and I can still explain every one of them
from memory, which is not something I can say about most documents I have signed.

## What I took from it

Three things, and I have used all of them since.

The first is that the way to make a large collection tractable is to change the unit of work, not
to speed up the existing unit. Faster document-by-document review would have been a rounding error.
Reviewing a haystack at a time was a different order of magnitude.

The second is that a visualization has to admit what it does not know. Zero Concepts, Limited
Concepts, Miscellaneous Files, sitting there in their own squares — that is a system telling the
truth about its own coverage, and I have never regretted building that into anything.

The third is the concept trash bin. The clustering was good, but the person looking at it always
knew something the clustering did not, and giving them a way to say so — drag the useless concept
off the compass, recluster, watch the map reorganise — is what made it a tool rather than a verdict.
Nearly twenty years later that is still the property I look for first in anything that puts a model
between a person and their data.
