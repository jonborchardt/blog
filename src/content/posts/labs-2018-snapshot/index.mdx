I left Oath in March 2018 and started at the Allen Institute for AI in May. In the gap I did what
you do: I went looking for the work I was proudest of, so I could show it to strangers. It was
scattered across old drives and dead intranets, so over a few weeks in February and March I copied
all of it into one directory, wrote a landing page over it, and called that my portfolio.

That directory has sat untouched ever since. I opened it again in 2026, expecting to spend a day
resurrecting it. It took one command. This post is the map of what is in there, and the start of a
series where I go through it one project at a time.

## What the snapshot actually is

A single folder of static files with a card grid on top of it — `default.htm`, split into
Products, Demos, and Patents. No build step, no bundler, nothing to deploy. Each card is a thumbnail
and a one-line description pointing at a gallery of screenshots, a video, or — for a good number of
them — the live thing itself.

The card copy is the pitch I was making to hiring managers in 2018, which is why it reads the way
it does. "Explore the TV landscape." "What industries should a network target?" I was not writing
for posterity. I was writing for a phone screen next Tuesday. The demos overview video sitting in
the folder was recorded in 2018 for the same job hunt, which makes it the closest thing this
archive has to a time capsule.

## Some of this was the job, and some of it nobody asked for

I want to be straight about which was which. The Bing work was my job. The ONE TV platform at
AOL was my job. Plenty of what is in this folder was work somebody requested and I shipped.

But the parts I would show you first were not assigned to anyone. DocMapper's visual ring, the
crossfilter and dc.js filtering approach, the PivotViewer Audience Planner, the folder of D3
demos — those I dreamt up and built in 20% time, and several of them stopped being side projects.
The Audience Planner, DocMapper, and sell-side targeting all became flagship products for the
company that employed me at the time. That is a strange ratio to look back on — a
scratch-an-itch demo becoming the thing the sales team leads with — and it happened often enough
here that I no longer think it was luck.

## Seventeen years, four employers

Almost everything in the folder was made at work, so the folder has the shape of a career: Attenex
from 2001 to 2007, straight out of school; Microsoft Bing from 2008 to 2010; PrecisionDemand, a TV
ad-tech startup, from 2010 to 2014; and then four years inside AOL, which acquired us in 2014 and
was renamed Oath around me in 2017. I made Principal there in 2016.

The through-line is not a technology. The early work is OpenGL and DirectX, the middle is
Silverlight, the late work is D3 and AngularJS, and every one of those choices looks dated now.
What actually persists is a habit of pointing a visualization at a pile of data nobody could read.

## Why it still runs in 2026

Because it is all static files, and static files do not rot the way applications do. Serving the
whole portfolio in 2026 is one command:

```sh
npx http-server . -p 8123 -c-1
```

Then open `default.htm`. Every internal link in the site is root-absolute, so the directory has to
be the web root — opening a file directly off disk breaks the styling and the navigation, which is
the only trap.

Two things are genuinely dead: the Silverlight client with its WCF backend, because no browser has
Silverlight any more, and an ASP.NET MVC explorer whose API was switched off years ago, though a
static equivalent of it survives in the D3 demo set. Everything else — the galleries, the
interactive dashboards, the videos — comes up mostly as it did, with the kinds of rot you would
expect of decade-old pages. Each post in this series covers its own project's damage.
