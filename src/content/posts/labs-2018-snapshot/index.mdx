I left Oath in March 2018 and started at the Allen Institute for AI in May. In the gap I did what
you do: I went looking for the work I was proudest of, so I could show it to strangers. It was
scattered across old drives and dead intranets, so over a few weeks in February and March I copied
all of it into one directory, wrote a landing page over it, and called that my portfolio.

That directory has sat untouched ever since. I opened it again in 2026, expecting to spend a day
resurrecting it. It took one command. This post is the map of what is in there, and the start of a
series where I go through it one project at a time.

## What the snapshot actually is

A single folder of static files with a card grid on top of it — `default.htm`, split into
Products, Demos, and Patents. No build step, no framework, no database. Each card is a thumbnail
and a one-line description pointing at a gallery of screenshots, a video, or — for a good number of
them — the live thing itself.

The card copy is the pitch I was making to hiring managers in 2018, which is why it reads the way
it does. "Explore the TV landscape." "What industries should a network target?" I was not writing
for posterity. I was writing for a phone screen next Tuesday.

## Seventeen years, four employers

Almost everything in the folder was made at work, so the folder has the shape of a career. Attenex
out of school, then Bing, then a TV ad-tech startup that got acquired, then four years inside the
acquirer while it was renamed around me.

The through-line is not a technology. The early work is OpenGL and DirectX, the middle is
Silverlight, the late work is D3 and AngularJS, and every one of those choices looks dated now.
What actually persists is a habit of pointing a visualization at a pile of data nobody could read
— and one metric.

## Everything here started as 20% time

This is the part I want to say plainly, because it is the reason the series exists at all: every
one of these tools was something I dreamt up and built in 20% time. None of them was assigned.

Several of them stopped being side projects. The Audience Planner, DocMapper, sell-side targeting,
and the whole approach of building highly interactive dashboards on crossfilter and dc.js all
became flagship products for the company that employed me at the time. That is a strange ratio
to look back on — a scratch-an-itch demo becoming the thing the sales team leads with — and it
happened often enough here that I no longer think it was luck.

## tRatio, the thing that never went away

There is a number called **tRatio** — targeting ratio — on screen in nearly every product in this
folder. It is in the 2011 Silverlight app, it is in the D3 rewrites, and it is still there in the
2018 ONE TV interface, several rewrites and two employers later.

A ratio compares two amounts: how much of one thing there is relative to another. tRatio was our
way of doing that consistently, so that two very different pieces of media could be put next to
each other and ranked on how well they hit the audience you were paying to reach.

I do not know who coined it, and I never found out why it was the one piece of vocabulary that
survived every rewrite. I have looked. It just kept getting carried forward, the way a load-bearing
word does.

## Why it still runs in 2026

Because it is all static files, and static files do not rot the way applications do. Serving the
whole portfolio in 2026 is one command:

```sh
npx http-server . -p 8123 -c-1
```

Then open `default.htm`. Every internal link in the site is root-absolute, so the directory has to
be the web root — opening a file directly off disk breaks the styling and the navigation, which is
the only trap.

Two things are genuinely dead. The Silverlight client with its WCF backend cannot run, because no
browser has Silverlight any more. An ASP.NET MVC explorer needs an API that was switched off years
ago, though a static equivalent of it survives in the D3 demo set. There is also a slow leak I did
not design for: several pages load scripts over plain `http://` from CDNs, and one of them, Google's
old `jsapi` charting library, has been retired outright, which kills two pages dead. Everything
else — the galleries, the interactive dashboards, the videos — comes up exactly as it did.

The demos overview video sitting in there was recorded in 2018 for the same job hunt. It is me
narrating my own work to an imagined interviewer, which makes it the closest thing this archive has
to a time capsule.

## The map of the series

Eleven posts follow this one, roughly chronological, one per project. I will link them here as
they go up:

- **Attenex DocMapper** — a 3D map of an electronic-discovery document collection, and the patents
  that came out of it.
- **Bing hover preview** — the hover preview panel, the quick-answer module and the core results
  list, on the Bing UX team.
- **BunnyPuddle games** — Android games shipped around 2010, including a water simulation built
  because water simulations are fun.
- **The Audience Planner** — the Silverlight app that became PrecisionDemand's flagship.
- **Media Foundry** — verifying that a TV spot actually aired, using watermarks and paid spotters
  across the country.
- **Google Charts to D3** — replacing off-the-shelf charts with our own, one demo at a time.
- **Sell-side targeting** — the dashboard that told a network which industries it should be
  chasing. Real customers, and eventually a patent.
- **TV data explorers** — the internal tools we built mostly to understand the data we already had.
- **One codebase, two datasets** — a personal project on public data, and the fork of it that
  became an internal rewards report.
- **ONE TV: upfront and scatter** — the platform I spent my last years at AOL and Oath on, in both
  its upfront and its scatter form.
- **ONE TV in Australia** — the same system against a different currency, different time zones and
  a different ratings body.

Some of these I remember in detail. Some I am reconstructing from screenshots and my own
eight-year-old card copy, and where my memory has a hole in it I would rather say so than fill it
in. Everything I could not verify gets flagged as such.
