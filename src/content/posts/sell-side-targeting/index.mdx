When I built the [portfolio I froze in 2018](/labs-2018-snapshot/), two of the cards on it were
almost the same picture. Both were dashboards full of bars and bubbles, built from the same pile of
scripts, running on the same TV ad data. The card copy is what separated them. One asked how well
an industry was buying its airtime. The other asked, of one hour-long block of airtime on one
station, which industry should be buying it.

That second question is one of the three things in this portfolio I am proudest of having asked. It
started as a 20% time idea, ended up in front of real customers, and eventually became a granted
patent with my name on it. But the first one is not the warm-up act, and I want to be careful about
that, because I was ready to describe it that way here, and it would not be true. Industry Ratings
was a real product with real customers and it is
still, eight years later, the more interesting thing to sit down and _use_.

## The transaction has two sides

A TV ad buy is a transaction, and every transaction can be looked at from either end of it.

The advertiser — or more usually its agency — is on the **buy side**. It has a product, a budget,
and an idea of who should be watching. Its question is: across all the airtime I bought last year,
how well did I actually reach those people, and did I overpay for the privilege?

The network is on the **sell side**. It has inventory: a specific station, on a specific day, in a
specific hour, running a specific program, with a specific audience sitting in front of it. Its
question is the mirror image: given this one slot, which advertiser's audience does it fit best,
and what should I be charging them for it?

The data underneath is identical. Airings, audiences, prices. What flips is which side of the join
you hold still.

Two dashboards came out of that, `IndustryRankings` and `sellSide`. They shared the same chart
library — a folder of scripts all prefixed `pd`, for PrecisionDemand, where I was the sole UI
developer from 2010 until AOL acquired us in 2014; everything on either screen is mine, design and
code.

## Two numbers to read the screens with

Every bar on both dashboards is a function of two numbers, so here they are up front.

The first is a targeting score — a stat of ours that correlated the audience the advertiser wants
with the audience the media actually delivered. Positive means the buy leans toward the right
people, zero means it is effectively random, and negative means it leans the wrong way — the
example in our own help text was senior life insurance advertised on Cartoon Network. It was
comparable across industries, which is what makes a leaderboard of unrelated industries a sane
thing to draw at all.

Around it sat two prices. **CPM** is cost per thousand impressions, standardized to a thirty-second
spot, and **tCPM** divides it by the targeting score — the price of a thousand usefully targeted
impressions, lower is better. Costs came from SQAD clearing rates, impressions from Nielsen, the
targeting from our own modelling.

## Buy side: how well is your industry buying?

`IndustryRankings` shipped under the name **PD Industry Ratings** — the navbar brand next to it
says _PD Industry Ranking_, singular, and nobody ever reconciled the two. Its headline was a
question posed directly to whoever was looking at it: _How well do the top agencies reach their
target audience?_

The top band is three horizontal bar charts sharing a single column of labels — targeting score,
tCPM, CPM, one row per industry. It reads like a leaderboard, but note the sort: the rows are ordered by
**tCPM ascending**, cheapest useful impressions first, and the score bars are left to fall where
they may. That was deliberate. The industry with the best targeting is not the industry getting the
best deal, and putting the two next to each other in tCPM order makes the mismatch impossible to
miss. Senior Life Insurance sits at the top with a score of 0.37 and a tCPM of $8.06. Music, three
rows down, has almost as good a score at 0.35 and pays more than twice as much for it.

Below the bars, two bubble charts do the same job geometrically: targeted impressions against
spend, and targeting score against tCPM, with bubble radius carrying a third variable. The help text told
you where to look — bottom and to the right is most efficient — and the bubbles that sat away from
the diagonal were the conversation.

Down the left rail are six histograms — score, tCPM, CPM, Spend, Impressions, tImps — and every
one of them is a filter. Click a bar, or drag across a range of them, and the whole page recomputes.
There is a dataset menu in the navbar with twenty industries in it plus a TV-overall set, so you
could either compare everyone or go and live inside one vertical.

And then across the bottom, the thing this whole post exists to show you: a strip of impressions
along the footer with a brush on it. Every dashboard I built in this era had one. Drag a window
across the time axis and everything above refilters — instantly, no submit button, no page load. I
think of it as the sweep, and it is the single interaction I would demo first if you gave me one
minute.

Load the demo above and drag the brush from 19 January to 10 November 2011 — same page, same
dataset, one gesture later. The interesting part is what does _not_ stay put:

The detail the table cannot carry is that Term's targeting _improves_ inside the window rather than
merely getting cheaper, and that Life Insurance Investment and Exercise Equipement — spelled the way
the product spells it — keep their labels but lose their bars entirely, because inside those ten
months they have no qualifying airings at all. None of it is a page load: it is the same
crossfilter-and-dc.js machinery [from the previous post](/drag-the-timeline-refilter-the-page/)
doing set arithmetic under your cursor, and it is the reason a static screenshot of any of this
work undersells it so badly.

## Sell side: one slot, twenty-three answers

Now turn the question around.

The header says `15,502 selected out of 15,502 records`, which is crossfilter's row count and the
first thing that changes when you touch anything. Down the left are Station, Day and Hour. Down the
right are the metrics: industry, targeting score and its delta, tCPM, Optimal CPM, CPM Delta, CPM Delta %,
Impressions — every one of them a filter, and a `Sort Cards by` toggle that flips the ranked cards
between alphabetical and score order.

The middle is the actual invention. Each row is one airing, and it starts with the facts: station,
day, hour, program, the brand currently in that slot and its industry. Then the money — current CPM,
optimal CPM, the delta between them, the revenue delta, the targeting-score delta. Then twenty-three columns
headed `1st` through `23rd`.

Those columns are the answer. For that one airing, all twenty-three industries in the model are
scored on how well their target audience matches the people actually watching, and the columns hold
them in ranked order — a pictogram for the industry, its fit score above it, and a background shaded
from yellow up to bright green by how good the fit is. Reading one row left to right, you get: here
is what is in this slot now, here is what it costs, here is what it _could_ cost, and here in order
are the twenty-three industries who should be bidding for it.

Take the row for _Ridiculousness_ on MTV, Monday 7am. It is currently running a mascara ad, and the
`Cur Indy` cell scores that industry's fit at $-0.03$ — effectively worse than random for this
audience. The two best-fitting industries score 0.39, the third and fourth drop off a cliff to 0.07,
and the delta column puts the gap at 0.41. Meanwhile the slot clears at \$6.80 CPM against a
modelled optimum of \$7.60.

That row is a sales call. There are exactly two industries worth phoning about this slot, the
mascara currently in it is not one of them, and the network is under-charging for it anyway.

Fifteen thousand rows of that is not a report. It is a worklist. And because it is crossfilter
underneath, a salesperson could click their own station, drag the score-delta histogram to just the
slots where the gap was largest, and be looking at their next twenty phone calls in a couple of
seconds.

## Twenty percent time, and then a patent

Nobody asked for this one. It came out of the time the company let us spend on our own ideas, and
it had the most obvious commercial edge of anything I built that way, because it was the only one
aimed at the side of the transaction that actually owns the
inventory.

It got built, it got in front of customers, and then it got written up. The application was filed
on 4 August 2015 as US 14/817,990, _Systems and Methods for Sell-Side TV Ad Optimization_. It
granted on 18 February 2020 as **US10567820B2**, with me as a co-inventor — nearly two years after
I had left the company, which is a very normal and very anticlimactic way for a patent to arrive.

<Callout variant="note" title="What I can and cannot tell you about the outcome">
  Both dashboards served real customers rather than being pure sales theatre — that much I am sure
  of. I do not have numbers for what either of them did commercially, and I am not going to
  reconstruct any. The patent is a matter of public record; the revenue is not something I ever saw.
</Callout>

## What still runs, eight years on

Both pages are static files, and I can still open both of them today. There is no build step and
never was — `sellSide` does not even have a project file. `IndustryRankings` is funnier: it ships
with a `.csproj`, a `Web.config`, and a `bin/` folder full of .NET 4.5 DLLs, and needs precisely
none of it. The page is plain HTML. Any static server will do.

What has rotted is entirely at the edges. Both pages pull jQuery over plain `http://` from a CDN,
which works on a local server and fails as mixed content on anything served over HTTPS. Both
reference `../libs/`, `../scripts/` and `../content/` in lowercase against folders actually named
`Libs`, `Scripts` and `Content` — invisible on Windows, a wall of 404s anywhere case-sensitive. The
copies embedded above are the exception: preserving them for this site meant patching exactly those
edges — a vendored jQuery, lowercased paths and filenames — and nothing else. And a handful of the
interactions are subtly buggy now in ways I did not notice at the time and am not going to fix in
2026.

The sweep still works, though. I drove the brush across it to make the screenshot above, and it
still repaints the whole page in one frame — which, after more than a decade and one acquisition, is
more than I expected from a folder of files I bulk-copied into a portfolio the month I quit.
