The brief was one sentence: an epic orchestral score, aliens coming to earth, not a battle but an
emotional battle of will, lots of instruments, designed rather than chaotic, fear and hope at once.

What came back was 64 BPM in D minor, eight sections, 59 bars, 62 parts, 28 distinct instruments, and
three minutes forty-one seconds. It is the piece I am proudest of out of this whole project, and it
has two bugs in it right now that I am going to show you.

<Callout variant="note" title="Everything here is one commit">
  Every number, clip and figure below describes the file at commit `7a4d1b0`, read on 2026-09-13.

  of a version rather than of the file.

## The idea that carries it

Two wills, side by side, that never merge.

The human side is a piano theme, with a harp and recorders behind it. It **follows the harmony**: the
same scale degrees, re-rooted on whatever chord is underneath, so it moves when the music moves.

The alien side is handchimes and tubular bells. They **do not follow**. The chimes play the same three
notes and the bells the same two, in the same rhythm, bar after bar, indifferent to the chords going
past.

The entire difference between following the harmony and ignoring it is one boolean:

```js
const theme  = { sound: 'piano',       follow: true,  notes: '0 ~ 2 4@2 ~ 5 4@2 0 ~ 2 4@2 ~ 7 6@2' };
const signal = { sound: 'handchimes',  follow: false, notes: '0 ~ ~ 6 ~ ~ 3 ~ ~ 0 ~ ~ 6 3 ~ ~' };
```

Hear it. Same four bars, same chords underneath, one part at a time:

    { src: alienContact, label: "the signal, not following" },

/>

The idea is implemented more than once. The human phrase is stretched over two bars and the alien
phrase is one, so the human breathes slower. The human line carries timing jitter, which the alien
line does not. And in the fifth section, where the key turns to D phrygian, the human theme shrinks to
a half-step sigh that *bends with the chord*, while the chimes sit exactly where they always were.

That jitter one is worth a caveat, because it is not really a second mechanism: the humanise control
does nothing at all below its midpoint, so writing zero on the alien parts is identical to leaving it
out. The checker still reports them as "very mechanical". The vocabulary can express an intention the
engine has no way to act on.

## Where the idea breaks, and it is a good break

There is one section, `plea`, that modulates to F major. And `follow: false` turns out to mean
something narrower than I thought.

It means "do not track the chords within a section". It does **not** mean "do not transpose with the
section's key". So when the key changed, the immovable signal moved. Its intervals, two stacked
perfect fourths everywhere else in the piece, become a fourth and a tritone:

<SoundBite
  label="The signal that never changes, changing"
  note="The same part in the first climax and then in the modulated section. Its interval is not the same."
  clips={[
    { src: alienContact, label: "in D minor: a stack of fourths" },
    { src: alienPlea, label: "in F major: a fourth and a tritone" },
  ]}
/>

Nothing in the system reported this, because nothing in the system knows the chimes were supposed to
be immovable. There is no way to express "these three absolute pitches, always" in the language; there
is only "these degrees, in this key". The composer's intent and the language's semantics come apart
at exactly the one place the key changes.

I am not going to fix it. It is the most alien-sounding moment in the piece, and it got there by
accident.

`void` is one bar: an organ chord and a drone, and that is the whole section. `signal` is the alien
alone, for eight bars, before anything human arrives. `approach` brings the theme in under a filter
that opens across the section. `contact` is the first climax. `will` drops to D phrygian with the bass
on a sustained didgeridoo. `plea` modulates to F major and is the one section the human side clearly
wins. `threshold` is the peak, twelve bars, thirteen parts. `after` is six bars of leaving.

### Who is winning

The brief said neither side wins at the climaxes. That is close to true, and the more interesting
thing is what happens in between.

Read per instrument rather than per side, the climaxes really are close: at the first, the piano leads
the chimes and bells by about 17 percent; at the peak, the piano and glockenspiel together lead them
by about 12. The human side wins on headcount,
not on level, because by then it has four voices against two.

But the arc between them is better than "neither wins". The alien opens alone. The human arrives.
Parity. The alien takes the fifth section. The human takes the sixth. Parity again at the peak. And
then the chimes get the last word.

## Fear low, hope high

The other half of the brief was a mixing instruction disguised as an image: fear in the low half, hope
in the high half. It is in the numbers, as a clean separation between the two groups' medians.

The separation is not luck, and it is not a hole in the pitch space either: plenty of individual
notes span the middle, and the strings alone cover both halves. What is separated is the *centre of
gravity* of each voice, because the register control moves a part by whole octaves rather than
smoothly. The language pushed the two groups apart.

The best detail in the piece is the one voice that crosses. The bowed strings start in the fear half,
climb into the hope half as the piece opens up, drop back down for the standoff in `will`, and drop
again at the finale. One part crossing the line eight times is the "both at once" idea living in a
single instrument.

Two corrections to my own header comment, though. The tubular bells are described as part of the
alien signal in one sentence and part of the hope-high group in the next, and by register they are the
**lowest** of the high voices: they alternate D3 and middle C, so half their notes sit below it. And
the pedal organ plays a D at about 18
hertz, from a sample library that bottoms out at 55. Every note is stretched down as much as
nineteen semitones. On a laptop speaker you are not hearing the fundamental at all; you are hearing
the harmonics and a very slow attack. Fear in the low half is partly fear below the speaker.

## Four rounds of being confidently wrong

After the first listen I reported a whistle at the start of the piece. What followed was four rounds
of diagnosis, and every one of them was wrong about the whistle. Two of them were argued in detail.

**Round one** blamed the wind bed and a bowed wineglass, and turned both down. Wrong.

**Round two** removed the wind and space beds from the opening entirely. Wrong, and I came back with
"the whistle you removed was wrong."

**Round three** blamed the reverb. This one is worth dwelling on because it was *correct about a
different bug*: the audio engine keeps one reverb per output and rebuilds its impulse response
whenever an event arrives with a different room size, and the pads carried four different sizes, so
the reverb buffer was being swapped under every piano strike. That got fixed, with a comment that is
still in the file. The whistle survived.

**Round four** rendered audio, ran a spectrum analysis, and produced the best paragraph in the entire
provenance file:

> rendered and measured, the void pair produced one naked 440 Hz line that each pad alone left faint
> and both doubled in phase. It is the sixth harmonic of the shared D2, the one partial surviving the
> 300 Hz lowpass. The psaltery sample map is an octave off and its strong third harmonic lands
> exactly there

That is specific, physically literate, internally coherent, and arrived at by measurement rather than
guessing. It swapped the string sound and rebuilt the opening pad as a triad.

It was also wrong.

### What actually found it

I said one thing that turned out to matter, and it was not about sound at all:

> still whistles at the start; gone when a section is pinned or a pad is muted

That is an architectural observation. Pinning a section and muting a part both rebuild the song from
its declarative sections, which **drops the hand-written textures entirely**. So the whistle had to be
in a texture, and no amount of spectrum analysis on the declarative parts was ever going to find it.

It was one sample name. A drone was written as `didgeridoo`, which resolves to variant zero, which is
a 0.7-second **bark**. Pitched down and repeated once a bar, a bark is a descending whoop.

```js
// :8 is a sustained note; :0 is a bark
```

Two minutes after the real cause was confirmed, a commit titled `song revert` undid the entire
spectrum-analysis theory: strings back to what they were, the opening pad back to its single low note.
Its recorded reason ends in five words that are exactly right: *those were not the whistle*.

<Callout variant="note" title="What the failure actually was">
  Not vagueness. The agent was *precise about the wrong thing* for four rounds, and the thing it never
  questioned was whether a name told it what a sound was. A name says nothing about its variants. My
  own diagnostic hint made it worse, because pin and mute drop the textures, so the two things I
  offered as clues were actively pointing away from the culprit.
</Callout>

And then the part I keep coming back to. The fix was not "be more careful". The fix went into the
tooling: the checker now prints the actual filename behind every sound it heard.

```text
  sounds
    didgeridoo:8             Didgeridoo1_Sus2_Main.wav (12 variants)
    timpani:0                Timpani1_Hit_v2_rr1_Sum.wav (30 variants)
    sus_cymbal:0             susCymb1_bow_13.wav (25 variants)
```

Four rounds of debugging became a permanent widening of what the agent can see. That is the only kind
of fix that compounds.

## The bug that is still in there

The reverb fix held. The other one did not, and I did it myself.

There were two pops, not one. The second was the reverb. The first was simpler: the melody part sends
to a delay line for any `space` value above 0.5, and the piano was at 0.75. At this tempo that echo
lands three-quarters of a beat behind each note, at twenty percent wet. It reads as an extra attack
that is not in the score.

The fix consolidated the piano to one setting for the whole piece, and added a comment at the top of
the file saying so. The comment is still there. The comment reads:

> The piano keeps one setting for the whole song: space .5 (room only; anything above sends it to a
> delay line, which reads as a pop behind each note)

An hour later, a commit called `fixes`, with no recorded reason and a one-word message, put the
per-section overrides back. In the shipped file the piano in two sections and the glockenspiel in a
third are on the delay again. Eighteen piano events and forty-two glockenspiel events carry it.

The comment and the code now disagree, and the comment is the one telling the truth about what was
intended. The provenance file stops recording at round eight; the last two commits to this song have
no entry at all.

That same undocumented commit left a second thing behind. It cut the opening section from two bars to
one and did not adjust the hand-written textures, which are sequenced separately and still add up to
sixty bars against the score's fifty-nine. So every gong, swell and heartbeat now lands **one bar
after** the section it was written for: the gong announcing the first climax fires a bar into it. And
because the two halves are different lengths, they drift by another bar every time the piece loops.
It is audible in the section clips above, and nothing in the system noticed, because nothing in the
system knows the textures were meant to line up with anything.

The session diary works right up until the moment a human opens the file and just fixes something.

## What it cannot do

I want to be clear-eyed about this, because the piece is better than my ability to defend it and
overselling it would cost more than the honesty does.

**There is no counterpoint.** The pads stack triads on the chord root and every voice moves in
parallel, every time. Inversions and voice leading are explicitly not modelled. A trained ear hears
parallel fifths across the whole piece, and the fifth section, where organ and strings and drone and
piano all step together, is parallel motion as the entire dramatic device.

**The development is one line, transposed, mostly unchanged.** One sixteen-step phrase carries the
entire human side. There are three further hand-written versions of it: a truncated sigh in the
phrygian section, a fragment in the closing bars, and the one that matters, the `answer`, which
changes the second half so it descends and lands on the root instead of hanging. That last one is a
single string edit, and it is the whole motivic argument the piece has.

**The rhythm cannot really change.** Density on a melody is a dice roll: below the midpoint it drops
notes at random, above it doubles them. In the fifth section it is set low enough that **two of the
eight bars have no human theme in them at all**. The section named for the human will asserting itself
has two bars where the human is simply absent, and that is a consequence of the undocumented revert.

**There is no tempo flexibility.** 64 BPM, exactly, for 221 seconds. Film scores live on rubato. This
has none.

**And the loudest single voice in the finale is the triangle.**

<SoundBite
  label="The drums at the peak"
  note="The triangle occupies the hi-hat slot, eight hits a bar, multiplied by the part's weight."
  clips={[{ src: triangleClimax, label: "threshold, drums only" }]}
/>

By summed gain it beats the strings, the harp, the pedal organ and the piano. The part's `weight` is
set high, weight multiplies every drum voice's gain, and the triangle is in the slot that fires most
often. A word that means "low end" made a triangle the loudest thing in the climax.

## What I actually think

Almost every round of feedback I gave was about sound quality: a whistle, a reverb, a pop. Two asked
for a section to be shorter and one asked for an architectural change. **Not one asked for different
notes.** I never requested a different melody, a different harmony, or a different shape. The
structure that came back from the first one-sentence brief is the structure that shipped.

So the honest summary is this. A trained listener would say it is a well-voiced, convincingly
orchestrated three and a half minutes with a real dramatic shape and one genuinely good idea about two
incompatible musics coexisting, built from one melodic cell repeated without development over four
chords repeated without variation, at one unwavering tempo. A very good sketch for a cue, played by a
very good sample library, by someone who has not yet learned to write a second phrase.

I still think the good idea is good. Two wills, in the same key, over the same chords, and neither one
gives way. It is expressed as a boolean, it survived eight rounds of me complaining about everything
except it, and it is audible to someone who cannot read music.
