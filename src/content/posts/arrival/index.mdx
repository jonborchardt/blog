The brief was one sentence: an epic orchestral score, aliens coming to earth, not a battle but an
emotional battle of will, lots of instruments, designed rather than chaotic, fear and hope at once.

What came back was 64 BPM in D minor, eight sections, 59 bars, 62 parts, 28 distinct instruments, and
three minutes forty-one seconds. This is what a piece written in the language from
[the first post](/a-language-on-top-of-strudel/) actually looks like, and this post is the honest
version: the one idea that carries it, the numbers behind its balance, the mixing pass it just got,
what it cannot do, and the two bugs that are still in the file.

## The idea that carries it

Two wills, side by side, that never merge.

The human side is a piano theme, with a harp and recorders behind it. It **follows the harmony**: the
same scale degrees, re-rooted on whatever chord is underneath, so it moves when the music moves.

The alien side is handchimes and tubular bells. They **do not follow**. The chimes play the same three
notes and the bells the same two, in the same rhythm, bar after bar, indifferent to the chords going
past.

The entire difference in pitch between following the harmony and ignoring it is one boolean:

```js
const theme  = { sound: 'piano',       follow: true,  notes: '0 ~ 2 4@2 ~ 5 4@2 0 ~ 2 4@2 ~ 7 6@2', ... };
const signal = { sound: 'handchimes',  follow: false, notes: '0 ~ ~ 6 ~ ~ 3 ~ ~ 0 ~ ~ 6 3 ~ ~', ... };
```

Hear it. Same four bars, same chords underneath, one part at a time:

  clips={[
    { src: humanContact, label: "the theme, following" },
    { src: alienContact, label: "the signal, not following" },

/>

The idea is implemented more than once. The human phrase is stretched over two bars and the alien
phrase is one, so the human breathes slower. And in the fifth section, where the key turns to D
phrygian, the human theme shrinks to a half-step sigh that *bends with the chord*, while the chimes
sit exactly where they always were.

Since the mixing pass there is a third difference, and it is a real mechanism rather than a dice
roll. The piano carries a `humanize` setting: every note lands up to twelve milliseconds late, by an
amount that follows a slow curve over the phrase and a fixed lean per beat, the same way every play.
The chimes carry nothing, and land exactly on the grid. The human side is played; the alien side is
sequenced. (The older `organicness` axis, which the chimes still set to zero, does nothing at or
below its midpoint, so writing zero there is identical to leaving it out. The checker still reports
them as "very mechanical", which happens to be right.)

## Where the idea breaks, and it is a good break

There is one section, `plea`, that modulates to F major. And `follow: false` turns out to mean
something narrower than I thought.

It means "do not track the chords within a section". It does **not** mean "do not transpose with the
section's key". So when the key changed, the immovable signal moved. Its intervals, two stacked
perfect fourths everywhere else in the piece, become a fourth and a tritone:

<SoundBite
  label="The signal that never changes, changing"
  note="The same part in the first climax and then in the modulated section. Its interval is not the same."
  clips={[
    { src: alienContact, label: "in D minor: a stack of fourths" },
    { src: alienPlea, label: "in F major: a fourth and a tritone" },
  ]}
/>

Nothing in the system reported this, because nothing in the system knows the chimes were supposed to
be immovable. There is no way to express "these three absolute pitches, always" in the language; there
is only "these degrees, in this key". The composer's intent and the language's semantics come apart
at exactly the one place the key changes.

I am not going to fix it. It is the most alien-sounding moment in the piece, and it got there by
accident.

`void` is one bar: an organ chord and a drone, and that is the whole section. `signal` is the alien
alone, for eight bars, before anything human arrives. `approach` brings the theme in under a filter
that opens across the section. `contact` is the first climax. `will` drops to D phrygian with the bass
on a sustained didgeridoo. `plea` modulates to F major and is the one section the human side clearly
wins. `threshold` is the peak, twelve bars, thirteen parts. `after` is six bars of leaving.

### Who is winning

The brief said neither side wins at the climaxes. That is close to true, and the more interesting
thing is what happens in between.

Read per instrument rather than per side, the climaxes really are close: at the first, the piano
leads the chimes and bells by about nine percent; at the peak, the piano and its vibraphone doubling
lead them by about one percent, and once the piano's velocity line is counted the alien side edges it.
The human side wins on headcount, not on level, because by then it has four voices against two.

But the arc between them is better than "neither wins". The alien opens alone. The human arrives.
Parity. The alien takes the fifth section. The human takes the sixth. Parity again at the peak. And
then the chimes get the last word.

## Fear low, hope high

The other half of the brief was a mixing instruction disguised as an image: fear in the low half, hope
in the high half. It is in the numbers, as a clean separation between the two groups' medians.

The separation is not luck, and it is not a hole in the pitch space either: plenty of individual
notes span the middle, and the strings alone cover both halves. What is separated is the *centre of
gravity* of each voice, because the register control moves a part by whole octaves rather than
smoothly. The language pushed the two groups apart.

The best detail in the piece is the one voice that crosses. The bowed strings start in the fear half,
climb into the hope half as the piece opens up, drop back down for the standoff in `will`, and drop
again at the finale. One part moving between the halves section by section is the "both at once"
idea living in a single instrument.

Two corrections to my own header comment, though. The tubular bells are described as part of the
alien signal in one sentence and part of the hope-high group in the next, and by register they are the
**lowest** of the high voices: they alternate D3 and middle C, so half their notes sit below it. And
the pedal organ plays a D at about 18 hertz, from a sample library that bottoms out at 55. Every note
is stretched down as much as nineteen semitones. On a laptop speaker you are not hearing the
fundamental at all; you are hearing the harmonics and a very slow attack. Fear in the low half is
partly fear below the speaker.

## The mixing pass

Everything above was true of the first finished version. What the file gained since is a mix, in the
sense [the next post](/mixing-as-a-scene/) is about: not louder and quieter, but a scene.

Measured before the pass, the loudest thing in the climax was the impact hit, five decibels under
the whole mix. The choir was a decibel louder than the piano theme it was meant to sit behind, and
shared the midrange with the organ. The timpani pulse was thirty decibels under the mix and the
glockenspiel doubling forty, which is to say neither could be heard. Nothing had a position, and the
parts were spread over five separate reverbs.

After it: one hall on the whole song, with each part's `space` value reduced to its send into that
hall. A position for every part, so the piano is in front and a little right, the chimes and bells
together on the right, the harp and recorders on the left, the doubling far left, the choir behind
and to the right and darker, so the organ keeps the low mids. The impact set back. The pulse turned
up to the top of its range because the timpani sample is quiet. The glockenspiel replaced by a
vibraphone, because the glockenspiel sample was too quiet to place at any level. The two spiky bell
strikes a bar given a compressor. And the piano and the pulse given the correlated `humanize`
described above in place of the old dice-per-hit jitter.

The measure tool's verdict went from five warnings to clean, and the mix's peak fell by more than
half, because the impact had been the peak. Two numbers from the pass are worth keeping because they cut against
intuition. First, by the score's own summed per-event gain the triangle is still the loudest voice in
the finale, by a factor of two and a half over the harp, because the pulse is at the top of its
level range and the triangle sits in the slot that fires most often; by the rendered measurement the
whole pulse sits twenty-one decibels under the mix. The number the language exposes and the number
the ear reports disagree, and the pass sided with the ear. Second, the piano's `weight: .58` that the
pass wrote is a no-op: the melody layer has no weight cell, the language accepts any axis on any
layer as documented, and the value sits in the file doing nothing.

## The bugs that survived it

Two things are wrong in the file as it stands, both were known before the mixing pass, and the
mixing pass touched neither. That is worth a paragraph, because the tools it used looked exactly
where they were pointed.

**The piano is on a delay line in two sections.** The header comment says the piano keeps one
setting for the whole song, with `space` held at or below the midpoint, because anything above that
sends the melody layer to a delay line that lands three-quarters of a beat behind each note and
reads as a pop. The shared definition obeys that (the header still quotes the old value, .5, while
the definition now says .32; both are under the line). Two per-section overrides do not: the piano in
`will` and in `after` is written well above the line, and so is the new vibraphone doubling in
`threshold`. Eighteen piano events and forty-two vibraphone events carry the delay.

The mixing pass measured `threshold`, where the piano is dry, and its recorded note even says the
header's rule still holds. It checked the constant and not the overrides forty lines down.

**The textures are one bar long.** Alongside the sections there are eleven hand-written Strudel
textures, gong swells and a wind bed and a heartbeat, sequenced separately, and they still add up to
sixty bars against the score's fifty-nine. So every gong, swell and heartbeat lands **one bar after**
the section it was written for: the gong announcing the first climax fires a bar into it. And because
the two halves are different lengths, they drift by another bar every time the piece loops. Nothing
in the system noticed, because nothing in the system knows the textures were meant to line up with
anything, and every tool that renders a section alone drops the textures entirely.

## What it cannot do

I want to be clear-eyed about this, because the piece is better than my ability to defend it and
overselling it would cost more than the honesty does.

**There is no counterpoint.** The pads stack triads on the chord root and every voice moves in
parallel, every time. The language now has inversions and a chord-tone follow mode; this piece uses
none of them, and voice leading is not modelled anywhere. A trained ear hears parallel fifths across
the whole piece, and the fifth section, where organ and strings and drone and piano all step
together, is parallel motion as the entire dramatic device.

**The development is one line, transposed, mostly unchanged.** One sixteen-step phrase carries the
entire human side. There are three further hand-written versions of it: a truncated sigh in the
phrygian section, a fragment in the closing bars, and the one that matters, the `answer`, which
changes the second half so it descends and lands on the root instead of hanging. That last one is a
single string edit, and it is the whole motivic argument the piece has.

**The rhythm cannot really change.** Density on a melody is a dice roll: below the midpoint it drops
notes at random, above it doubles them. In the fifth section it is set low enough that **two of the
eight bars have no human theme in them at all**. The section named for the human will asserting itself
has two bars where the human is simply absent.

**There is no tempo flexibility.** 64 BPM, exactly, for 221 seconds. Film scores live on rubato. This
has none.

## What I actually think

A trained listener would say it is a well-voiced, convincingly orchestrated three and a half minutes
with a real dramatic shape and one genuinely good idea about two incompatible musics coexisting,
built from one melodic cell repeated without development over four chords repeated without
variation, at one unwavering tempo. A very good sketch for a cue, played by a very good sample
library, by someone who has not yet learned to write a second phrase.

I still think the good idea is good. Two wills, in the same key, over the same chords, and neither one
gives way. It is expressed as a boolean, and it is audible to someone who cannot read music.
