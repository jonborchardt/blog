I joined PrecisionDemand in March 2010 as the sole user interface developer, and I was still the
sole user interface developer when AOL bought us in May 2014. Four years, one frontend person.
Every screen a customer or a salesperson or an analyst ever looked at was mine — the design
decisions as well as the code.

The Audience Planner is the one I would show you first. It started as a 20% time idea, it became
the company's flagship product, and it is one of the three things in [the portfolio I froze in
2018](/labs-2018-snapshot/) that I am proudest of. It is also completely dead: it was a Silverlight
application, and no browser has run Silverlight for years. What survives is a folder of screenshots
and a walkthrough video. That turns out to be enough to reconstruct it, because the product was
never complicated. It was four moves.

## What PrecisionDemand sold

TV ad-spend optimization. The pitch, more or less as I would have said it in an elevator: we could
tell you what market your ad dollars would convert into real sales.

That is a harder claim than it sounds. Television inventory is sold on audience size — a rating, a
CPM, how many eyeballs. The thing an advertiser actually cares about is whether the people watching
are the people who might buy a mid-size SUV. Our whole business was the gap between those two
things, and the Audience Planner was the interface where a buyer got to see the gap and act on it.

<Callout variant="note" title="Every number in these screenshots is fake">
  The advertisers, the programs, the ratings, the dollar figures — all of it is demo data, built to
  show the product. Nothing here is a real client's media plan.
</Callout>

## The whole product is four moves

Explore, filter, optimize, export. You open every program we had data on as one visual collection,
you narrow it with facets until you are looking at the inventory you care about, you hand the
survivors to the optimizer, and you leave with a spreadsheet.

Nothing about that flow was novel in 2011 and nothing about it is novel now. What made it work was
that all four moves lived in one window, over the same collection, with the counts and the averages
updating in the status bar as you went. You never left to run a report.

Here is the entry view. Each program in the catalogue is a card, and the cards are stacked into a

The status bar along the bottom is the part I would defend hardest today. Program Count: 2473.
Average Impressions: 241,772. Average Cost: $1,022.77. Average CPM: $4.23. Average tCPM: $112.12.
Whatever you had filtered down to, those five numbers described it, and they moved the instant you
touched anything. It is the cheapest possible feedback loop and it did more work than any chart on
the screen.

The left panel is the facet list: a search box, then the targeting score with a two-handle range
slider over its own miniature distribution, then three score clusters, then impressions per airing, targeted
impressions per airing, cost per airing, CPM, tCPM, and — further down the scroll — media market,
network, genre, conversions, cost per acquisition, primary daypart and primary day of week. The
breadcrumb at the top left says `tRatio: Over -0.4`, which is the filter that produced this view.

The toolbar has two dropdowns that I think are the most important controls in the product: **Sort**
and **Color**. Both are set to the targeting score here. You can point either one at any field independently,
which means the same collection can be arranged by cost and shaded by targeting quality, or the
reverse, and the answer to "am I overpaying for the wrong audience" falls out of the picture rather
than out of a calculation.

## The sort key and the colour

The number doing the sorting is a targeting score — a stat of ours that measured how well a
program's audience matched the audience the advertiser was paying to reach.

It let two very different pieces of media be put next to each other and ranked. In this app it is doing three jobs at once: it
bins the histogram, it sorts the cards, and it colours them on a red-through-yellow-to-green ramp.
The axis runs from about -0.4 to 0.5 with the slider going down to -1.

The `t` prefix elsewhere in the UI means "targeted" — you get `CPM` and `tCPM` as a pair, and
`Imps per Air` and `tImps per Air` as a pair. A program with a good CPM and a terrible tCPM is
cheap for a general audience and expensive for yours. Most of the product is about noticing that.

Every tile is a program, sized by whatever the sort dropdown is pointed at — impressions per airing,
here — coloured by targeting score, with its network's logo drawn on it. The genre facet on the left is showing its counts — 762 documentary, 191
instructions and advice, 149 general drama, 95 feature film, 62 news, 55 popular music, 45 situation
comedy, 31 sports — and `INSTRUCTIONS, ADVICE` is the one that is ticked, so the status bar now
reads 191 programs at an average of $1,632.80 an airing.

The logos were a deliberate choice and I would make it again. A buyer does not think in program
identifiers; they think "the Food Network block is cheap and the Travel Channel block is orange".
Putting the brand mark on the tile turned a data structure into something a media planner could
read across a conference table. Sort by impressions per airing, as it is here, and the big
recognisable names sort themselves to the top left without anyone explaining the layout.

## One card, opened up

Zoom into a single tile and it becomes a card with its own metrics, plus a detail panel. This is a
third session — back on the card grid rather than the treemap, with 2,328 programs in scope — so do
not try to reconcile its numbers with the two screenshots above.

Three things worth pointing at here.

The four metrics on the card each carry a tiny bar showing where that value sits in the whole
distribution. tCPM $48.02 is meaningless on its own; tCPM $48.02 with a marker near the good end of
the range is a decision. That pattern — never show a number without showing its context — is the one
idea on this screen that has not dated at all.

The **Airings** block lists a maximum weekly airing count and then a row per campaign, four vehicle
names and one called `PrecisionDemand Optimal`. That last row is the tell: the app is not only
describing the inventory, it is holding our own recommended plan alongside whatever the client is
actually running, ready to be compared.

The **Demographics** block is a list of household attributes — year the home was built, gender,
income, marital status, and, my favourite, `Golf - True` — each with a small diverging bar showing
whether this program's audience over- or under-indexes on it. The Silverlight-era backend that
survives on disk connects to two databases, one called `Demographics` and one called `Acxiom`, and I
would bet this panel is reading the second. Either way it is exactly where the business was: the
reason we could talk about sales instead of eyeballs is that somebody had joined TV viewing to
household data, and this panel is where that join surfaced to a human.

## Optimize: "Re-calculate Optimal Plan"

The other end of the product is a grid, and it is deliberately boring.

  <img
    src={optimizer.src}
    width="1075"
    height="805"
    alt="A dense data grid with columns for Lock, Optimal Airings, Program Airings, Program Duration, Program Name, Network, Cost per Air, Optimal Spend and Imps per Air, with buttons Re-calculate Optimal Plan and Export to Excel beneath and a pager reading Page 1 of 12"
  />
</Figure>

Columns: a lock checkbox, optimal airings as a numeric spinner with a bar beside it, the program's
own airing count, its duration, its name, its network, cost per airing, optimal spend, impressions
per airing. Twelve pages of it. Two buttons at the bottom: **Re-calculate Optimal Plan** on the
left, **Export to Excel** on the right.

The interesting thing about this screenshot is how much of it is zero. Of the twenty-eight rows
visible, six have a non-zero optimal airing count. Property Virgins, NY Ink, How I Met Your Mother,
Mutant Planet, My First Place: nothing. The plan puts fifty airings into The Tonight Show, eighteen
into House, forty-one into Cubs Baseball, fifty into Say Yes to the Dress, eighteen into So You
Think You Can Dance and twenty-six into 60 Minutes. An optimizer that concentrates is doing its job;
an optimizer that spreads a little money over everything is just computing an average. Watching most
of the grid stay at zero was how you knew the run had actually solved something.

I checked the arithmetic again while writing this, more than a decade later, and it survives: on all
six rows, optimal spend is optimal airings times cost per airing. Not exactly — fifty airings of The
Tonight Show at the displayed $19,893.62 comes to $994,681.00, and the cell says $994,681.10 — but
every row lands within fifteen cents, which is what you get when the per-airing cost on screen is
itself rounded to the penny and the real one is not.

The `Lock` column is the one piece here I am reading rather than remembering. A checkbox next to an
editable airing count, on a screen whose main verb is "re-calculate", is almost certainly there so a
buyer can pin the lines they have already committed to and let the solver move everything else
around them. I believe that is what it did. I would not swear to it.

## Export, and the comparison charts

The last move is a button labelled **Export to Excel**, and I have made peace with that.

Nobody bought a media plan out of a Silverlight window. They bought it out of a spreadsheet that
went to their agency, got argued over, and came back with changes. The most sophisticated part of
the product handed off to the least sophisticated format available, on purpose, because that is
where the work continued. A visualization tool without an escape hatch is a tool that asks people to
do their real work somewhere it cannot see.

Before the spreadsheet there was a comparison view.

The headlines are written as comparisons rather than values: "Airings will be 417 % more than
Client", "Targeted CPM will be 99 % less than Client", "Targeted impression will be 6,966 % more
than Client". Those are demo numbers and they are shameless demo numbers — a real proposal did not
promise a 99% CPM cut — but the framing is right. A buyer does not need to know the targeted CPM.
They need to know whether it is better than what they are doing now.

Each grouped chart has five bar series and no legend anywhere in the screenshot. The card detail
panel lists exactly five airing rows, four campaigns plus `PrecisionDemand Optimal`, which is
suggestive. I would not put money on the mapping.

The `Cable Impressions` card in the bottom right is drawing an empty axis — labelled, gridded, and
carrying no bars at all. A demo dataset with nothing in one of its buckets, most likely. I am not
cropping it out, because a portfolio that only shows the panels that filled in is not a portfolio,
it is a brochure.

## Why Silverlight, and what is left of it

Because the company started at the dawn of Silverlight. That is the whole answer. In 2010 it was a
credible bet: you got a real widget toolkit, real data binding, a real type system and a control
that already did faceted card browsing, at a time when doing any of that in a browser meant writing
it yourself. We pivoted away later as other web technology became dominant — not on a particular
Tuesday when someone slammed a door, just steadily, the way a platform stops being a decision and
starts being a liability.

The Planner's own source did not make it into the archive, but a sibling from the same period and
the same hands did, and it is short enough to read in one sitting.

<VizFigure
  name="The surviving Silverlight-era stack"
  summary="Three tiers and nothing else: a Silverlight .xap running as a browser plug-in, a single WCF endpoint called DalService.svc, and two SQL databases named Demographics and Acxiom on a server named baker. None of the three tiers can run today."
>
  <StackViz class="mx-auto h-auto w-full max-w-2xl" aria-hidden="true" />
</VizFigure>

A `.xap` file — a Silverlight client, zipped assemblies, still sitting on disk and still perfectly
decompilable — plus one WCF service, `DalService.svc`, plus a `Web.config` with Entity Framework
connection strings hardcoded to `data source=baker` and the catalogues `Demographics` and `Acxiom`.
Four dated backup copies put that directory between December 2011 and February 2012.

The service contract is three operations wide. `GetClusterSources`, `GetClusters`, and
`GetRelevantDemographicsOfCluster` — and there is the whole thesis, in an interface definition. Pick
a data source, get its clusters of households, ask which demographic attributes make a given cluster
distinctive. Those clusters are the same word as the `tRatio Cluster 1`, `2` and `3` facets in the
first screenshot, and that last method is the panel I just walked you through. The assemblies are
branded MarketScale, a name that appears nowhere else in the portfolio.

Three tiers, and every one of them is unreachable now. No browser ships the plug-in. `baker` was
decommissioned so long ago that I could not tell you what happened to it.

## What happened to it

AOL acquired PrecisionDemand in May 2014, and the quality of the platform was a key factor in that
decision. The Audience Planner was the piece of the platform people meant.

Then I killed it. On the other side of the acquisition I led the migration off Silverlight and onto
AngularJS, which is to say rebuilding my own flagship in a technology that could survive. The
rewrite was the right call, and the treemap was still better than anything that replaced it for
years.

## What I would keep

The technology is a museum piece and I would not defend a single line of it. Four decisions I would
port straight into anything I build tomorrow:

- **A status bar that never lies.** Five aggregates over the current filter, updating live. Cheaper
  than any dashboard and more trusted than all of them.
- **Sort and colour as independent, user-chosen dimensions.** Two dropdowns turned one view into
  every view.
- **Never a number without its distribution.** The little bar next to $48.02 is what made the $48.02
  actionable.
- **An export button.** The tool is not where the decision gets made. Let the work leave.

And the thing that is really worth saying: none of this was assigned. It was a 20% time project that
turned into the flagship. I have gone on to build a great deal of software that somebody asked for,
and the ones I would show you first are still the ones nobody did.
