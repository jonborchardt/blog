In 2013 I was the only UI developer at PrecisionDemand, a TV ad-tech startup, which meant every
chart our customers looked at was a chart I had put there. Most of them were not mine. They were
Google's, loaded off `google.com/jsapi`, configured with a few lines and shipped. That year I spent
my 20% time replacing them with D3 charts I wrote myself, and the folder of demos that came out of
that is still sitting in [the portfolio I froze in 2018](/labs-2018-snapshot/).

This is the post about that swap: what the off-the-shelf chart did, what I built to replace it, and
the one interaction, dragging a window across a timeline and watching an entire page of charts
recompute, that turned out to matter far more than the charts did.

## The chart I was replacing

Two files still sit unlinked at the root of the portfolio, `fordsuvindustry.htm` and
`jonbtest.htm`. They are the "before" artifacts, and they are about as simple as a 2013 chart page
got:

```html
<script type="text/javascript" src="http://www.google.com/jsapi"></script>
<script type="text/javascript">
  google.load("visualization", "1", { packages: ["annotatedtimeline"] });
  google.setOnLoadCallback(drawChart);
</script>
```

Under that, a couple of thousand hardcoded `data.addRows([...])` lines: a date, two targeting-score values,
and four spare string columns per row, an annotation title and its body text for each of the two
series. A number our own modelling produced was being drawn by a component I did not write and
could not change.

It is a good chart. Two series, lettered annotations pinned to dates with a panel explaining them,
zoom presets, and a range selector along the bottom. In 2013 Google drew all of that with a Flash
object, which is the detail that explains the headline on the page I replaced it with: "Replacement
for existing Flash based Industry Tracker". The Flash in that sentence was never ours. It was
Google's.

<Callout variant="note" title="These pages were supposed to be dead">
  I went in expecting a blank screen. The old `jsapi` loader is long deprecated and I had written
  these two pages off. They render fine. `google.load` now forwards to
  `gstatic.com/charts/51/loader.js`, which quietly draws the modern SVG `annotationchart` instead:
  twelve `<svg>` elements on the page and not one `<object>` or `<embed>`. So the screenshot above is
  Google's replacement for its own Flash chart, standing in for the thing I was actually looking at
  in 2013.
</Callout>

## Building the equivalent

Did the demo set win an argument inside the company? I don't remember one, and I am not going to
invent one. What I have is the files, and the files are unusually clear about the intent: this was not a chart inspired by Google's, it was a copy
of Google's with the ceiling taken off.

The replacement chart is `nv.models.PdLineWithFocusChart`, 795 lines registered straight into NVD3's
model namespace. `Pd` for PrecisionDemand. Two siblings sit next to it, `PdBubbleMap` and
`PdHeatMap`. And the data file it eats gives the game away:

```json
{
  "date": 1270105200000,
  "value": -0.044918704,
  "label": "A",
  "description": "A mixed bag of winners and losers",
  "notes": "DISC (Dirty Jobs & Mythbusters), DIY, NFLN, SPD and TRAV are well targeted, ..."
}
```

A label, a headline and a body per point. The label is new: Google auto-lettered its own markers,
there was no column for it. The other two are a rename of Google's pair: `description` is Google's
title column, and `notes` is Google's text column, which the Ford data mostly left unused. The series
colours are pinned in the same
file to `#4684EE` and `#DC3912`, the same blue and the same red the Google chart drew. Someone
opening the new page was not supposed to notice a new chart library. They were supposed to notice it
did more.

The zoom presets are gone, replaced by a full-width strip of the same two lines underneath the main
chart. You drag a window across that strip and the chart above it becomes that window. It is a
smaller idea than "1h 1d 5d 1w 1m 3m 6m 1y max" and a much better one, because the window is
continuous and you can slide it rather than pick it off a menu. The annotations stay pinned to
their dates while the window closes around them:

## The sweep

Here is the interaction the whole thing was really about.

Crossfilter holds one flat table in memory (for the rotations page, one row per TV rotation buy) and
lets you declare dimensions over it: purchase date, network, commission rate, creative length,
geography. Every chart on the page is a group over one of those dimensions. Filter any dimension and
every other group recomputes, incrementally, fast enough that you can do it inside a `mousemove`.

So the timeline strip is not a zoom control. It is a filter, and dragging it refilters the page.

Drag across the lower strip; the count above the charts is
crossfilter doing set intersection under your cursor:

Nothing on that page is a page load. The network ranking reorders, the commission-rate histogram
collapses onto two bars and spreads back out, the creative-length split flips between 30-second and
60-second spots, and the circles on the county map move, all of it while the mouse button is still
down.

The tracker gets the same treatment: its strip is the same kind of filter, which is why sweeping it
rescales the chart instead of zooming a picture.

## The demo set

The tracker and the rotations page were two pages of five. `demos/d3Demo` holds `industryTracker`, `rotationExample`,
`programExample`, `explorerExample` and `webSpike`, plus two hub pages. The hubs are the part I had
forgotten and now like the most. `demos.html` is six escalating throwaways (hover, animation on
click, more advanced animation, charting, animated charts, thousands of animated points), the
sketchbook of somebody learning a library in public. And `index.html`, titled "Front-end data
visualization examples", is not a demo at all. It is a page of links: d3js.org, nvd3.org,
crossfilter, four New York Times interactives, Bostock's Wealth & Health of Nations. That page is
the actual pitch. The demos underneath it are the evidence I could build that.

The stack under the demos was D3 v3 with NVD3 for the chart models, Square's crossfilter for the
filtering, dc.js on the explorer page, and topojson for the maps. The three pages not already
embedded above are preserved here too:

One file, `crossfilterChart.js`, opens with a comment I am still glad I wrote:

```js
// A direct copy from the crossfilter example:
// https://github.com/square/crossfilter/blob/50227903a5567d24c0d38141a5e94b5dec932b4c/index.html
```

Attribution, pinned to an exact commit, in the file itself. Thirteen years later it is the only
reason I can tell you with certainty where Square's example ended and my code began.

## What it turned into

The charts themselves did not survive. D3 v3, NVD3 and a hand-rolled `nv.models.Pd...` are not how I
would draw this today, and the pages that used them were rewritten more than once after AOL acquired
us in May 2014 and I moved the product off Silverlight and onto AngularJS.

The interaction survived. When I ask myself which of these side projects went anywhere, the list is
the Audience Planner, DocMapper, sell-side targeting, and then, oddly for a list of products, "using
crossfilter and dc.js for highly interactive charting". The technique itself became flagship. The
industry-ratings and sell-side dashboards that real customers used were built on this exact model:
click anything to filter everything, drag the timeline to refilter live. Those are their own posts.

## How it holds up in 2026

It runs. `npx http-server . -p 8123 -c-1`, open `/demos/d3Demo/rotationExample.html`, and the sweep
above is what you get. Those frames are from this month, not from 2013. The caveats are the ones you
would expect of a thirteen-year-old page:

- D3 and topojson load from `http://d3js.org`, so the demos work over a local `http` server and
  would break as mixed content on any `https` rehost.
- The sweep works; some of the other interactions are flaky now, and I have left them that way.

The Google chart still renders too, which I did not expect. But it renders because Google kept
maintaining it and silently swapped its guts out from under my page. Mine renders because, once it
was written, it never needed anything from anyone.
